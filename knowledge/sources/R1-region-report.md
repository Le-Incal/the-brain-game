# Brain Game Knowledge Base: Neuroscience Research Report (as of October 2026)

The core facts for all 20 game regions can be stated from textbook consensus, and the main risks for the guides are a few popular myths and fast-changing statistics, not gaps in the anatomy. This report gives sourced, paraphrased findings for each section. Every number that changes over time carries a date stamp. Every disputed claim is flagged.

## TL;DR

- Use these anchor numbers: about 86 billion neurons (Azevedo et al. 2009, J Comp Neurol; individual brains ranged from 78.8 to 95.4 billion), about 69 billion of them (roughly 80%) in the cerebellum, about 16 billion in the cerebral cortex, and a whole-brain glia-to-neuron ratio of roughly 1:1, not 10:1.\[1\]
- For Region 15, choose the midbrain over the piriform cortex. The midbrain shows up clearly on the inferior and midsagittal views and carries many teachable stories (Parkinson's disease, reward, eye-movement nerves). The piriform cortex is tiny, mostly hidden and hard to highlight. Teach smell's "no thalamus first" fact as part of the orbitofrontal and inferior-view narration instead.
- Fast-moving facts as of late 2026: stroke caused about 7.25 million deaths worldwide in 2021 (GBD 2021, via the WSO Global Stroke Fact Sheet 2025).\[2\] Lecanemab got traditional FDA approval on July 6, 2023,\[3\] and donanemab was approved on July 2, 2024.\[4\] Neuralink reported 21 implanted participants on January 28, 2026, with no peer-reviewed human data yet.\[5\] Adult human hippocampal neurogenesis is still debated, even after Dumitru et al. 2025 (Science).

## Key Findings

- **Neuron and glia counts.** Azevedo et al. 2009 counted 86.1 ± 8.1 billion neurons and about 85 billion non-neuronal cells in four adult male brains, aged 50 to 71.\[1\]\[6\] That is a small sample, so give the figure as "about 86 billion" with a range.\[7\] Lent et al. 2012 (Eur J Neurosci) note that "100 billion" falls outside the measured range.\[8\] Without the cerebellum, the rest of the brain has a higher glia-to-neuron ratio, up to about 4:1.\[9\]\[10\]
- **Broca's area.** Dronkers et al. 2007 (Brain 130:1432-1441) scanned the preserved brains of Leborgne and Lelong with MRI. Both lesions reached deep into medial regions, well beyond the surface area Broca described.\[11\]\[12\] So "Broca's aphasia" needs more damage than the classic area alone.\[12\]
- **Motor homunculus.** Gordon et al. 2023 (Nature 617:351-359) reported that the classic motor strip is interrupted.\[13\] Regions for foot, hand and mouth alternate with three "inter-effector" regions, which together form the somato-cognitive action network (SCAN). An open review from the Diedrichsen lab disputes how the boundaries are read.\[14\] Treat SCAN as a strong, recent proposal, not settled textbook fact.
- **Prefrontal maturation.** Gogtay et al. 2004 (PNAS) showed that higher-order association cortex matures last, but the sample only covered ages 4 to 21.\[15\] The popular "25" figure is an extrapolation. Mousley et al. 2025 (Nature Communications; 4,216 people aged 0 to 90) found the strongest whole-brain wiring turning point at about age 32.\[16\]\[17\] That study measured whole-brain topology, not the prefrontal cortex. Best-supported wording: maturation continues through the twenties and possibly into the early thirties, depending on what is measured.
- **Glymphatic system.** Xie et al. 2013 (Science) reported that sleep enlarged interstitial space by about 60% and sped clearance in mice.\[18\] Miao et al. 2024 (Nature Neuroscience) found the reverse: brain clearance was "markedly reduced, not increased" during sleep and anesthesia, also in mice.\[19\] The Nedergaard group has published a rebuttal.\[20\] Label the glymphatic system as debated.
- **"Time is brain."** Saver 2006 (Stroke 37:263-266) estimated that a typical untreated large-vessel ischemic stroke destroys about 1.9 million neurons, 14 billion synapses and 12 km of myelinated fibers every minute.\[21\]

## Section 1. The 20 Game Regions

Format for each region: what it is, how it functions, connections, what it controls, numbers, subregions and Brodmann areas (BA), blood supply, damage, kid fact, expert detail, myths, pronunciation, sources. Visibility tells the camera which view shows the region best.

### 1. Prefrontal cortex (PFC)
- **What:** The front part of the frontal lobe, in front of the premotor areas. It is the most anterior cortex.
- **Function:** Executive control. This covers working memory, planning, rule switching, inhibiting impulses, and valuing choices.
- **Connections:** Dense two-way links with the mediodorsal thalamus, basal ganglia (caudate), parietal and temporal association cortex, amygdala, hippocampus and cingulate.
- **Controls:** Goal-directed behavior and self-regulation. It does not move muscles directly.
- **Subregions:** Dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC, BA 9 and 46), ventrolateral PFC (BA 44, 45 and 47), ventromedial PFC (vmPFC, medial BA 10, 11, 25 and 32), dorsomedial PFC (medial BA 8 and 9), frontopolar cortex (BA 10).
- **Visibility:** Anterior, lateral, superior and medial views. The vmPFC sits on the medial and inferior surfaces. It is mostly on the surface, with sulci (superior and inferior frontal sulci) dividing its gyri.
- **Blood supply:** Middle cerebral artery (MCA) supplies the lateral convexity. Anterior cerebral artery (ACA) supplies the medial and superior parts.
- **Damage:** Dysexecutive syndrome, perseveration (getting stuck on a rule, as in the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test) and apathy (DLPFC). vmPFC damage causes poor real-life decisions with intact IQ. Examples include Damasio's patient EVR and the Iowa Gambling Task deficits behind the somatic marker hypothesis. Phineas Gage's injury mainly hit the left vmPFC and orbitofrontal cortex.
- **Kid fact:** The PFC is like the brain's team captain. It helps you wait your turn and plan a game.
- **Expert/open questions:** Is the PFC organized along a rostro-caudal abstraction gradient? How does dopamine tune PFC working memory along an inverted U? Frontopolar BA 10 is relatively enlarged in humans compared with apes (Semendeferi et al. 2001).
- **Myths:** "Fully mature at 18" and "fully mature at exactly 25" are both oversimplified (see Section 12). "The frontal lobe is the personality center" is too simple, because personality depends on distributed networks.
- **Lateralization:** The right ventrolateral PFC is often linked to response inhibition (Aron et al. 2004), with moderate evidence. There is no "logical" hemisphere.
- **Pronunciation:** prefrontal pree-FRUN-tul; dorsolateral dor-soh-LAT-er-ul.
- **Sources:** Kandel, Principles of Neural Science, 6th ed., chapters on the prefrontal cortex; Blumenfeld, Neuroanatomy through Clinical Cases, chapter 19 (frontal lobes); Purves, Neuroscience.

### 2. Orbitofrontal cortex (OFC)
- **What:** The underside of the frontal lobe, lying just above the eye sockets (orbits).
- **Function:** Represents the expected value of rewards and punishments. It updates choices when outcomes change (reversal learning). It combines smell, taste and visual food signals into flavor value.
- **Connections:** Amygdala, ventral striatum, hypothalamus, insula, piriform and other olfactory cortex (direct), mediodorsal thalamus, and inferotemporal cortex.
- **Controls:** Value-based decisions, social behavior and emotion regulation.
- **Subregions:** BA 11, 13, 14 and 47/12 (Price and Carmichael scheme). Medial and lateral OFC differ in function. The gyrus rectus and medial orbital, lateral orbital, anterior orbital and posterior orbital gyri are arranged around the H-shaped orbital sulcus.
- **Visibility:** Inferior (ventral) view only. The olfactory bulbs and tracts run along its medial edge, in the olfactory sulcus.
- **Blood supply:** ACA (medial orbitofrontal branches) and MCA (lateral orbitofrontal branches).
- **Damage:** Disinhibition, impulsivity, socially inappropriate behavior, and impaired reversal learning. It is often damaged in traumatic brain injury, because the orbital surface scrapes over the bony floor of the skull. Olfactory groove meningiomas here can cause loss of smell and personality change.
- **Kid fact:** The OFC helps you decide whether a snack is worth it. It "knows" that the tenth cookie is less exciting than the first.
- **Expert detail:** The OFC may encode a "cognitive map" of task states (Wilson et al. 2014, Neuron), not just value. Human OFC is a secondary olfactory area, and some think the right OFC is key for conscious smell perception.
- **Myths:** That the OFC is just "the reward center." Reward depends on a network that includes the ventral striatum and the VTA.
- **Pronunciation:** orbitofrontal OR-bih-toh-FRUN-tul; gyrus rectus JY-rus REK-tus.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Purves; Rolls, review literature on OFC and reward.

### 3. Premotor cortex (PMC)
- **What:** The lateral part of BA 6, just in front of the primary motor cortex.
- **Function:** Prepares and selects movements, especially those guided by external cues such as vision. It holds action plans. Mirror neurons were first found in monkey ventral premotor area F5 (Rizzolatti and colleagues, 1990s).
- **Connections:** Primary motor cortex, posterior parietal cortex (reaching and grasping circuits), PFC, cerebellum (via thalamic VL), basal ganglia, and the spinal cord (some direct corticospinal fibers).
- **Controls:** Reaching, grasping, cue-triggered action and movement sequences.
- **Subregions:** Dorsal premotor (PMd) and ventral premotor (PMv). The frontal eye field (FEF, around BA 8 and the junction of the precentral and superior frontal sulci) sits nearby and controls voluntary eye movements (saccades).
- **Visibility:** Lateral view, mostly in front of the precentral sulcus. Part of it lies in the sulcal banks.
- **Blood supply:** MCA (superior division).
- **Damage:** Motor planning deficits, limb-kinetic apraxia. FEF damage causes the eyes to deviate toward the side of the lesion in the acute phase. Lesions that reach premotor areas add spasticity to weakness.
- **Kid fact:** The premotor cortex is the "get ready" area. It plans your catch before your hand moves.
- **Expert detail:** The human mirror neuron system is still contested. Claims linking it to empathy and autism are much weaker than claims about action understanding (Hickok 2009, J Cogn Neurosci).
- **Pronunciation:** premotor pree-MOH-tur.
- **Sources:** Kandel (voluntary movement chapters); Purves; Rizzolatti and Craighero 2004, Annu Rev Neurosci.

### 4. Supplementary motor area (SMA)
- **What:** The medial part of BA 6, on the inner (medial) surface of the frontal lobe, in front of the leg area of M1.
- **Function:** Starts self-generated movements, sequences actions, coordinates the two hands, and supports the urge or intention to move. The pre-SMA, just in front, handles choosing and switching between actions.
- **Connections:** M1, premotor cortex, cingulate motor areas, basal ganglia (the putamen, via thalamic VA/VL), and the spinal cord.
- **Controls:** Internally generated, well-learned movement sequences, such as playing a memorized song.
- **Subregions:** SMA proper and pre-SMA, divided by the vertical line through the anterior commissure (VCA line).
- **Visibility:** Medial view mainly. A small strip reaches the superior surface.
- **Blood supply:** ACA.
- **Damage:** SMA syndrome after surgery: temporary loss of movement initiation (akinesia), sometimes with mutism, which usually recovers over weeks. Left SMA lesions can cause transcortical motor aphasia. Medial frontal lesions can cause alien limb behavior.
- **Kid fact:** The SMA is your inner starter pistol. It says "go!" when you decide to move all by yourself.
- **Expert detail:** The Bereitschaftspotential, or readiness potential (Kornhuber and Deecke 1965), and the Libet experiments implicate SMA activity before the conscious decision to move. Their meaning for free will is still debated.
- **Pronunciation:** Bereitschaftspotential beh-RYTE-shafts-poh-TEN-see-ahl.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Blumenfeld; Nachev, Kennard and Husain 2008, Nat Rev Neurosci (SMA and pre-SMA).

### 5. Primary motor cortex (M1)
- **What:** The precentral gyrus (BA 4), just in front of the central sulcus (the fissure of Rolando).
- **Function:** Sends commands that set the force and direction of voluntary movement. Population coding of movement direction was described by Georgopoulos et al. 1982.
- **Connections:** Sends the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts. Receives input from premotor cortex, SMA, S1, and the thalamus (VL, relaying cerebellar and basal ganglia loops).
- **Controls:** Voluntary movement on the opposite side of the body, especially fine finger control.
- **Key cells:** Betz cells, giant pyramidal neurons in layer V and among the largest neurons in the human nervous system. They make up only a small fraction of corticospinal axons (estimates are often given as about 3 to 5%).
- **Motor map:** The motor homunculus runs, from medial to lateral, leg (on the medial surface, paracentral lobule), trunk, arm, hand, face, then tongue and larynx (lateral, near the Sylvian fissure). Gordon et al. 2023 proposed the SCAN revision (see Section 3).
- **Subregions:** BA 4a (anterior) and 4p (posterior, in the sulcus) (Geyer et al. 1996, Nature).
- **Visibility:** Lateral and superior views, continuing onto the medial surface as the paracentral lobule. Much of BA 4 is buried in the anterior wall of the central sulcus.
- **Blood supply:** MCA for face, arm and hand. ACA for the leg and foot area (medial).
- **Damage:** Weakness of the opposite side (contralateral hemiparesis) with upper motor neuron signs: hyperreflexia, a Babinski sign and later spasticity. ACA strokes weaken the leg more than the arm. MCA strokes weaken the face and arm more than the leg.
- **Kid fact:** The motor strip is like a piano keyboard for your body. Each key moves a different part.
- **Expert detail:** M1 carries rotational population dynamics during reaching (Churchland et al. 2012, Nature). In primates, direct cortico-motoneuronal connections underlie fine finger independence.
- **Myths:** That M1 is a perfect, continuous body map. Body-part maps overlap and are flexible.
- **Pronunciation:** precentral pree-SEN-trul; Betz BETS; Rolando roh-LAN-doh.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Purves; Nolte, The Human Brain; Penfield and Rasmussen 1950.

### 6. Broca's area
- **What:** The pars opercularis (BA 44) and pars triangularis (BA 45) of the inferior frontal gyrus, usually in the left hemisphere.
- **Function:** Speech production planning, sequencing of speech sounds, syntactic processing, and selecting words and meanings under competition. Modern data show it is functionally mixed. Part of it belongs to the language network and part to the domain-general "multiple demand" network (Fedorenko et al. 2012, Current Biology).
- **Connections:** The arcuate fasciculus and superior longitudinal fasciculus (dorsal stream) to posterior temporal cortex. The extreme capsule and uncinate (ventral stream) to temporal cortex. The frontal aslant tract to the SMA and pre-SMA. Also connects to ventral premotor and motor face areas.
- **Controls:** Fluent, grammatical speech. It works with motor cortex for articulation.
- **History:** Paul Broca presented the case of Louis Victor Leborgne ("Tan") in 1861. Leborgne could produce essentially only the syllable "tan." A second patient, Lelong, followed. In 1865 Broca concluded that "we speak with the left hemisphere." Dronkers et al. 2007 MRI showed that both patients' lesions reached into deep medial regions, including the superior longitudinal fasciculus in Leborgne, beyond the surface lesion Broca saw.
- **Lateralization (real):** Language is left-dominant in about 95% or more of right-handers and about 70% of left-handers. The exact percentage varies by method and study (Knecht et al. 2000, Brain, using Doppler ultrasound).
- **Visibility:** Lateral view, left frontal lobe, just above the anterior Sylvian fissure. The pars opercularis partly covers the insula.
- **Blood supply:** MCA superior division.
- **Damage:** Broca's (non-fluent, expressive) aphasia: effortful, telegraphic speech, relatively spared comprehension of simple sentences, and frequent right-sided weakness. A lesion confined to BA 44/45 usually causes milder or temporary deficits. Lasting Broca's aphasia needs larger lesions (Mohr et al. 1978, Neurology). Apraxia of speech has been linked to the insula (Dronkers 1996, Nature) and to premotor cortex.
- **Kid fact:** Leborgne was nicknamed "Tan" because that was the main word he could say, even though he understood a lot.
- **Expert detail:** Broca's area contains subregions that differ in receptor architecture (Amunts et al. 2010). The right homolog supports prosody and helps recovery after left-hemisphere damage.
- **Myths:** "Broca's area = speaking, Wernicke's area = understanding." This is too simple (see Region 13).
- **Pronunciation:** Broca BROH-kuh; pars opercularis parz oh-PER-kyu-LAIR-iss; pars triangularis parz try-ang-gyu-LAIR-iss; Leborgne leh-BORN-yuh.
- **Sources:** Dronkers et al. 2007, Brain; Blumenfeld, chapter 19; BrainFacts.org, "Struggling to Speak: The Brain Map of Patient Tan" (2016).

### 7. Primary somatosensory cortex (S1)
- **What:** The postcentral gyrus, just behind the central sulcus. It contains BA 3a, 3b, 1 and 2.
- **Function:** First cortical stage for touch, vibration, pressure, body position (proprioception), and part of pain and temperature.
- **Connections:** Input from the thalamus: VPL carries body signals and VPM carries face signals. Output goes to S2 (parietal operculum), posterior parietal cortex (BA 5 and 7) and M1.
- **Subregions:** 3a handles proprioception from muscle spindles. 3b is the core touch area and the "true" S1. Area 1 handles texture. Area 2 handles size and shape (combining touch and position).
- **Visibility:** Lateral and superior views, continuing medially onto the paracentral lobule. BA 3a and 3b are buried in the posterior wall of the central sulcus. Area 1 is on the crown, and area 2 is on the anterior wall of the postcentral sulcus.
- **Blood supply:** MCA for face and arm, ACA for the leg.
- **Damage:** Loss of fine touch and position sense on the opposite side, astereognosis (cannot identify objects by touch), agraphesthesia (cannot identify numbers traced on the skin), and poor two-point discrimination. Crude pain and temperature sensation often remain.
- **Kid fact:** Your fingertips and lips have so much "brain space" that you can feel a tiny grain of sand with them.
- **Expert detail:** Each Brodmann subarea contains its own separate body map. Ongoing research examines how S1 maps change with use and amputation (see Section 3).
- **Pronunciation:** somatosensory soh-MAT-oh-SEN-suh-ree; postcentral post-SEN-trul; astereognosis ay-STAIR-ee-og-NOH-sis.
- **Sources:** Kandel (touch chapters); Purves; Nolte.

### 8. Superior parietal lobule (SPL)
- **What:** The upper part of the parietal lobe, behind the postcentral gyrus and above the intraparietal sulcus (IPS).
- **Function:** Combines vision with body position to guide reaching, grasping and eye movements. It maintains the body schema, shifts spatial attention, and manipulates mental images (mental rotation).
- **Connections:** S1, visual dorsal stream areas, premotor cortex, FEF, PFC, and the precuneus medially.
- **Subregions:** BA 5 (anterior) and BA 7 (7A and 7P). The intraparietal areas (AIP, LIP, MIP and VIP, mapped mostly in monkeys) line the IPS.
- **Visibility:** Superior, lateral and posterior views. It continues medially into the precuneus.
- **Blood supply:** MCA (posterior parietal branches). It lies near the ACA and PCA border zones, so it is vulnerable in watershed (border-zone) strokes.
- **Damage:** Optic ataxia (misreaching under visual guidance). Bilateral parieto-occipital damage causes Balint syndrome: simultanagnosia, optic ataxia and oculomotor apraxia. It also contributes to neglect, especially on the right side.
- **Kid fact:** The SPL is your body's GPS. It tells your hand where the cup is so you don't knock it over.
- **Expert detail:** Core node of the dorsal attention network (Corbetta and Shulman 2002).
- **Pronunciation:** parietal puh-RYE-uh-tul; lobule LOB-yool.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Blumenfeld, chapter 19 (parietal lobes); Corbetta and Shulman 2002, Nat Rev Neurosci.

### 9. Angular gyrus (AG)
- **What:** BA 39, in the inferior parietal lobule, curving around the end of the superior temporal sulcus. The supramarginal gyrus (BA 40) lies just in front of it.
- **Function:** A hub where several types of information meet. It supports semantic memory and combining concepts, reading, arithmetic fact retrieval, recollection of episodic memories, attention reorienting, and awareness of one's own body (agency).
- **Connections:** Default mode network partners (precuneus, PCC and mPFC), language areas via the arcuate fasciculus and middle longitudinal fasciculus, visual cortex, and the hippocampal system.
- **Visibility:** Lateral view, posterior parietal area. It is mostly on the surface.
- **Blood supply:** MCA inferior division (angular artery).
- **Damage:** Left side: Gerstmann syndrome (agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia and left-right confusion) and alexia with agraphia (Dejerine 1891). Right side: contributes to spatial neglect. Stimulation near the right angular gyrus once produced an out-of-body experience (Blanke et al. 2002, Nature; a single case).
- **Kid fact:** The angular gyrus helps turn squiggles on a page into words that mean something.
- **Expert detail:** Seghier 2013 (The Neuroscientist) frames the AG as a cross-modal integrative hub. Whether "pure" Gerstmann syndrome exists, and whether its four signs reflect damage to white matter tracts rather than cortex, is debated.
- **Myths:** "Einstein's genius came from his large parietal lobes." This is a single-case, after-the-fact interpretation (Witelson et al. 1999, Lancet). Present it as an anecdote, not proof.
- **Pronunciation:** angular ANG-gyu-lur; Gerstmann GERST-mun; supramarginal SOO-pruh-MAR-jih-nul.
- **Sources:** Seghier 2013; Blumenfeld; Kandel.

### 10. Precuneus
- **What:** The medial part of the parietal lobe (medial BA 7), between the paracentral lobule in front and the cuneus behind (separated by the parieto-occipital sulcus).
- **Function:** Visuospatial imagery, recall of episodic memories, self-referential thought, and taking first-person perspective. It is highly active at rest.
- **Connections:** Posterior cingulate cortex, SPL, thalamus (pulvinar and lateral posterior nucleus), PFC, and parahippocampal cortex.
- **Visibility:** Medial view only.
- **Blood supply:** ACA (precuneal branches of the pericallosal artery) and PCA (parieto-occipital branch).
- **Damage:** Isolated lesions are rare. It is one of the earliest regions to show reduced metabolism and amyloid build-up in Alzheimer's disease. It also changes in states of altered consciousness, such as anesthesia and the vegetative state.
- **Kid fact:** The precuneus helps you picture your bedroom with your eyes closed.
- **Expert detail:** Cavanna and Trimble 2006 (Brain) is the classic review. The precuneus is a core DMN hub with very high resting metabolism.
- **Pronunciation:** precuneus pree-KYOO-nee-us; cuneus KYOO-nee-us.
- **Sources:** Cavanna and Trimble 2006, Brain; Raichle et al. 2001, PNAS.

### 11. Primary auditory cortex (A1)
- **What:** The transverse temporal gyri (Heschl's gyri, BA 41, with surrounding belt BA 42) on the upper surface of the temporal lobe, buried inside the lateral (Sylvian) fissure.
- **Function:** First cortical analysis of sound: pitch, timing and loudness. It is laid out as a tonotopic (frequency) map.
- **Connections:** Input from the medial geniculate nucleus (MGN) of the thalamus. Output to belt and parabelt cortex (STG, planum temporale), then to ventral "what" and dorsal "where/how" auditory streams toward the frontal lobe.
- **Visibility:** Not visible on the intact surface. It appears only if the Sylvian fissure is opened or the frontal and parietal operculum is removed. On the model, show it by "lifting" the frontal lip or by pointing into the fissure.
- **Blood supply:** MCA inferior division (temporal branches).
- **Damage:** A one-sided lesion causes little hearing loss, because each ear projects to both hemispheres. Bilateral lesions cause cortical deafness or auditory agnosia.
- **Lateralization (real, modest):** Left auditory cortex is somewhat better at fast timing (speech). Right is somewhat better at fine pitch (music) (Zatorre et al. 2002). These are tendencies, not a strict split.
- **Kid fact:** Your hearing cortex is hidden inside a deep fold, like a secret room.
- **Expert detail:** People often have one or two Heschl's gyri (duplications are common). Its size has been linked to musical training (Schneider et al. 2002, Nature Neuroscience).
- **Pronunciation:** Heschl HESH-ul; tonotopic toh-noh-TOP-ik.
- **Sources:** Kandel (auditory chapters); Purves; Nolte.

### 12. Superior temporal gyrus (STG)
- **What:** The top gyrus of the temporal lobe (BA 22, plus 41 and 42 on its upper surface). It includes the planum temporale and borders the superior temporal sulcus (STS).
- **Function:** Speech sound perception (phonemes) and auditory object recognition. The right STG and STS process prosody (the "music" of speech). The posterior STS processes biological motion, faces and voices, and supports social perception.
- **Social cognition and sarcasm:** Understanding sarcasm requires matching what words say against tone and context. Lesion studies link sarcasm deficits to right-hemisphere and vmPFC damage (Shamay-Tsoory, Tomer and Aharon-Peretz 2005, Neuropsychology). In neurodegenerative disease, failure to detect sarcasm from tone of voice tracks with right posterior temporal (including STS) and temporal pole atrophy (Rankin et al. 2009, NeuroImage). The nearby temporoparietal junction (TPJ) supports theory of mind (Saxe and Kanwisher 2003). Sarcasm is a network job, not one spot.
- **Connections:** A1, MTG, angular and supramarginal gyri, Broca's area (arcuate fasciculus), and the amygdala and insula (voice emotion).
- **Visibility:** Lateral view, along the lower edge of the Sylvian fissure. The planum temporale and Heschl's gyri are buried.
- **Blood supply:** MCA inferior division.
- **Damage:** Bilateral damage causes pure word deafness. Right-side damage causes receptive aprosodia (missing emotional tone; Ross 1981). Left posterior damage contributes to Wernicke's aphasia.
- **Lateralization (real):** The planum temporale is larger on the left in most people (Geschwind and Levitsky 1968, Science).
- **Kid fact:** The STG helps you tell when someone says "great job" and means "oops."
- **Pronunciation:** planum temporale PLAY-num tem-puh-RAH-lee; prosody PROSS-uh-dee.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Shamay-Tsoory et al. 2005; Rankin et al. 2009; Hickok and Poeppel 2007, Nat Rev Neurosci.

### 13. Wernicke's area
- **What:** Traditionally the posterior part of the left STG (posterior BA 22). Some definitions add parts of BA 39, 40, 21 and 42.
- **Function:** Speech comprehension and mapping sounds to meaning. Modern work splits the roles. The posterior STG and STS handle phonological processing and help monitor speech output. Middle and inferior temporal regions handle word meaning.
- **History:** Carl Wernicke, in 1874, described patients with fluent but meaningless speech and poor comprehension. He also predicted conduction aphasia from disconnection.
- **Boundary debate:** A survey of experts found no agreement on where Wernicke's area is (Tremblay and Dick 2016, Brain and Language, which suggested retiring the "Broca-Wernicke" model). Binder 2015 (Neurology) argued that the comprehension deficits in Wernicke's aphasia come mostly from damage to middle temporal and surrounding cortex, and that the posterior STG mainly supports phonological retrieval.
- **Connections:** Arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area. Also connects to the angular gyrus, MTG and A1.
- **Visibility:** Lateral view, posterior left STG, near the end of the Sylvian fissure.
- **Blood supply:** MCA inferior division.
- **Damage:** Wernicke's (fluent, receptive) aphasia: fluent speech with paraphasias and neologisms, poor comprehension, and often unawareness of the problem (anosognosia). Usually there is no weakness. There may be a superior quadrantanopia (Meyer's loop).
- **Kid fact:** With Wernicke's aphasia, people can talk smoothly, but the words come out jumbled, and it's hard for them to understand others.
- **Myths:** That Wernicke's area is a single, sharply bounded "understanding center."
- **Pronunciation:** Wernicke VER-nih-kuh (in English, often WER-nih-kee).
- **Sources:** Binder 2015, Neurology; Tremblay and Dick 2016; Blumenfeld.

### 14. Fusiform gyrus
- **What:** A long gyrus on the underside of the temporal and occipital lobes (mostly BA 37, part of BA 20), between the collateral sulcus (medial) and the occipitotemporal sulcus (lateral).
- **Function:** High-level ventral "what" vision: faces, bodies, written words, objects and color (with the lingual gyrus).
- **Named areas:** The fusiform face area (FFA, Kanwisher, McDermott and Chun 1997, J Neurosci) is stronger on the right. The visual word form area (VWFA, Cohen and Dehaene and colleagues, around 2000) is in the left occipitotemporal sulcus and lateral fusiform. The mid-fusiform sulcus marks a boundary between face-selective (lateral) and place-selective (medial) cortex.
- **Connections:** Early visual areas (V1 to V4), the inferior longitudinal fasciculus to the anterior temporal lobe, the amygdala, and language areas (VWFA).
- **Visibility:** Inferior view only.
- **Blood supply:** PCA (temporal and occipital branches).
- **Damage:** Prosopagnosia (face blindness) after right or bilateral damage. Pure alexia, or letter-by-letter reading, after left VWFA damage. Central achromatopsia (loss of color vision) after damage to ventral occipitotemporal cortex. Developmental prosopagnosia affects an estimated 2 to 2.5% of people (Kennerknecht et al. 2006), with estimates varying by criteria.
- **Lateralization (real):** Face processing is right-biased. The VWFA is left-biased. Learning to read pushes face responses further toward the right (Dehaene et al. 2010, Science).
- **Kid fact:** A part of your brain gets excited whenever it sees a face, even a face in a cloud or an electrical outlet.
- **Expert detail:** Is the FFA face-specific or an expertise area? The "Greeble" debate (Gauthier) is unresolved.
- **Pronunciation:** fusiform FYOO-zih-form; prosopagnosia PROS-oh-pag-NOH-zhuh.
- **Sources:** Kanwisher et al. 1997; Dehaene and Cohen 2011, Trends Cogn Sci; Purves.

### 15. Unresolved candidate region: piriform cortex versus midbrain

**(a) Piriform cortex (primary olfactory cortex)**
- **What:** Three-layered paleocortex at the junction of the frontal and temporal lobes, near the limen insulae and over the uncus and amygdala. It is not a standard Brodmann neocortical area.
- **Function:** Identifies and remembers odors. It combines receptor input into odor "objects." Odors are coded by scattered, distributed groups of neurons (ensembles) rather than a tidy map.
- **Connections:** Receives the lateral olfactory tract directly from mitral and tufted cells of the olfactory bulb, with no thalamic relay first. Projects to the OFC both directly and through the mediodorsal thalamus, and to the amygdala and entorhinal cortex.
- **Visibility:** Inferior view, a small strip near the stem of the lateral fissure. It is largely hidden by the temporal pole and frontal lobe.
- **Blood supply:** Branches of the MCA and the anterior choroidal artery.
- **Damage:** Isolated lesions are rare. Loss of smell usually comes from the nose, bulb or olfactory nerve. Piriform cortex is part of epilepsy-prone circuits in temporal lobe epilepsy.
- **Kid fact:** Smell takes a shortcut. It goes straight to the smell cortex without stopping at the brain's main relay station first.

**(b) Midbrain (mesencephalon)**
- **What:** The top part of the brainstem, between the pons and the diencephalon.
- **Parts:** The tectum (roof) holds the superior colliculi (visual orienting, eye movements) and inferior colliculi (auditory relay). The tegmentum holds the red nucleus, the oculomotor (III) and trochlear (IV) nuclei, the periaqueductal gray (PAG; pain control, defensive behavior, vocalization), the ventral tegmental area (VTA; dopamine for reward and motivation), and the reticular formation. The substantia nigra (pars compacta: dopamine to the striatum; pars reticulata: basal ganglia output) sits between the tegmentum and the cerebral peduncles. The cerebral aqueduct runs through it.
- **Cranial nerves:** CN III exits in front, in the interpeduncular fossa. CN IV is the only nerve that exits the back of the brainstem, and its fibers cross.
- **Visibility:** Inferior view (cerebral peduncles and interpeduncular fossa), midsagittal medial view (tectum, aqueduct, tegmentum) and a posterior view if the cerebellum is cut away (colliculi).
- **Blood supply:** PCA and basilar tip perforators, superior cerebellar artery, and posterior choroidal arteries.
- **Damage:** Weber syndrome (CN III palsy plus weakness of the opposite side). Parinaud (dorsal midbrain) syndrome, with impaired upward gaze, often from pineal tumors. Parkinson's disease (loss of nigral dopamine neurons). Hydrocephalus if the aqueduct narrows.
- **Kid fact:** If a ball flies at your head, your midbrain makes you look and duck before you even think.

**Recommendation:** Paint the midbrain. It is bigger, visible from three views, and connects to many player questions: Parkinson's, reward, eye movements, "why do I flinch?" Keep the piriform cortex as a spoken "hidden region" fact when the camera shows the inferior view near the OFC and temporal pole. This also keeps the important myth correction about smell and the thalamus.

- **Pronunciation:** piriform PEER-ih-form; mesencephalon mez-en-SEF-uh-lon; colliculus koh-LIK-yu-lus; colliculi koh-LIK-yu-lye; tegmentum teg-MEN-tum; substantia nigra sub-STAN-shuh NYE-gruh; periaqueductal pair-ee-ak-weh-DUK-tul.
- **Sources:** Kandel (smell chapter; brainstem); Purves; Blumenfeld, chapter 14 (brainstem); Gottfried 2010, Nat Rev Neurosci (olfactory cortex).

### 16. Cingulate cortex
- **What:** A belt of cortex wrapped around the corpus callosum on the medial surface (cingulum means "belt").
- **Function and subregions (Vogt's model):**
  - Anterior cingulate (ACC; BA 24, 32, 33 and subgenual 25): emotion, autonomic control, detecting conflict and errors, the unpleasant feeling of pain, and motivation. The subgenual part has been a deep brain stimulation target in depression research (Mayberg et al. 2005), with mixed trial results.
  - Midcingulate (MCC; BA 24' and 32'): action selection, cingulate motor areas, pain.
  - Posterior cingulate (PCC; BA 23 and 31) and retrosplenial cortex (BA 29 and 30): DMN hub, self-referential thought, memory and navigation.
- **Connections:** Amygdala, insula, PFC, thalamus (anterior and mediodorsal nuclei), hippocampus (through the cingulum bundle) and the motor system.
- **Visibility:** Medial view only. It lies above the callosal sulcus and below the cingulate sulcus.
- **Blood supply:** ACA (pericallosal and callosomarginal arteries). The posterior parts are also supplied by PCA branches.
- **Damage:** Bilateral ACC damage can cause akinetic mutism or severe apathy. Cingulotomy reduces the distress of pain more than its intensity. PCC hypometabolism is an early Alzheimer's sign.
- **Kid fact:** The cingulate is your brain's "uh-oh" detector. It notices when you make a mistake.
- **Pronunciation:** cingulate SING-gyu-lut; subgenual sub-JEN-yoo-ul; retrosplenial reh-troh-SPLEE-nee-ul.
- **Sources:** Vogt 2005, Nat Rev Neurosci; Kandel; Blumenfeld.

### 17. Primary visual cortex (V1)
- **What:** Striate cortex (BA 17) along the calcarine sulcus of the occipital lobe. It is named for the stria of Gennari, a white stripe visible to the naked eye.
- **Function:** First cortical stage of vision. Neurons detect edge orientation, motion direction, binocular disparity and color contrasts (Hubel and Wiesel; Nobel 1981).
- **Retinotopic map:** Each hemisphere maps the opposite visual field. The upper field maps below the calcarine sulcus (lingual gyrus), and the lower field maps above it (cuneus). The center of vision (fovea) is hugely magnified at the occipital pole.
- **Connections:** Input from the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) through the optic radiations. Output to V2, V3, V5/MT, the superior colliculus, the pulvinar and back to the LGN (feedback).
- **Visibility:** Medial view, along the calcarine sulcus. A small part is visible at the posterior occipital pole. About two-thirds of V1 is buried inside the calcarine sulcus.
- **Blood supply:** PCA (calcarine artery). The occipital pole often also gets MCA branches, which helps explain "macular sparing" after PCA strokes.
- **Damage:** Loss of the opposite half of the visual field in both eyes (contralateral homonymous hemianopia), often with macular sparing. Bilateral damage causes cortical blindness. Anton syndrome is cortical blindness with denial of blindness. Blindsight is unconscious visual responses in the blind field (Weiskrantz).
- **Numbers:** Human V1 is roughly 20 to 30 cm² per hemisphere and varies about threefold between people (Dougherty et al. 2003, J Vis; Benson et al.).
- **Kid fact:** You "see" with the back of your head. Your eyes are the cameras, but the picture is made at the back of your brain.
- **Pronunciation:** calcarine KAL-kuh-ryne; striate STRY-ate; Gennari jeh-NAR-ee; retinotopic ret-in-oh-TOP-ik.
- **Sources:** Kandel (visual chapters); Purves; Nolte.

### 18. Visual association cortex (V2, V3, V4, V5/MT)
- **What:** Extrastriate cortex (BA 18 and 19, plus parts of the temporal and parietal lobes) surrounding V1.
- **Areas:** V2 (BA 18) handles contours and figure-ground. V3 handles global form and motion. V4 (ventral occipital) handles color and shape (Zeki). V5/MT (lateral occipitotemporal junction) handles motion.
- **Two streams (Ungerleider and Mishkin 1982; Goodale and Milner 1992):**
  - Ventral "what" stream: V1 to V2 to V4 to inferotemporal cortex and fusiform. Recognition.
  - Dorsal "where/how" stream: V1 to V2/V3 to MT to posterior parietal cortex. Spatial location and visually guided action.
  - The streams interact heavily. The two-stream model is a useful simplification.
- **Visibility:** Posterior, lateral, medial and inferior views.
- **Blood supply:** PCA. The lateral occipital and MT region also lies in MCA territory and border zones.
- **Damage:** Achromatopsia (V4 region). Akinetopsia, or motion blindness, as in patient L.M. (Zihl et al. 1983) with bilateral lesions near MT. Visual form agnosia, as in patient D.F. (ventral stream), who could not name shapes but could post a card through a slot accurately. Balint syndrome (dorsal stream).
- **Kid fact:** One brain patch sees color, and another sees movement. One woman with damage to her motion area saw pouring tea as a frozen icicle.
- **Expert detail:** Primate visual cortex contains dozens of areas (about 30 in macaques; Felleman and Van Essen 1991). Human homologs beyond V1 to V3 remain debated.
- **Pronunciation:** extrastriate ek-struh-STRY-ate; akinetopsia ay-kin-eh-TOP-see-uh; achromatopsia ay-kroh-muh-TOP-see-uh.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Goodale and Milner 1992, Trends Neurosci; Zeki, review literature.

### 19. Cerebellum
- **What:** The "little brain" under the occipital lobes, behind the brainstem. It is about 10% of brain mass but holds about 69 billion neurons, roughly 80% of the total (Azevedo et al. 2009). Most are tiny granule cells.
- **Function:** Timing, coordination, balance, eye-movement control and motor learning. It predicts the results of movement and corrects errors. It also supports cognition and emotion.
- **Cognitive and affective roles:** Schmahmann and Sherman 1998 (Brain) described the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (CCAS). It involves executive dysfunction, impaired spatial cognition, language problems (agrammatism, reduced verbal fluency) and blunted or disinhibited affect, mainly after posterior lobe lesions. Lateral posterior lobes (Crus I and II) link to prefrontal and parietal association cortex. Buckner et al. 2011 found every major cortical network mapped in the cerebellum.
- **Anatomy:** Vermis (midline) and two hemispheres. Anterior, posterior and flocculonodular lobes. Lobules I to X. Deep nuclei: fastigial, interposed (globose and emboliform) and dentate. Cortical layers: molecular, Purkinje and granular. Purkinje cells are the only output of the cerebellar cortex and are inhibitory (GABA). Climbing fibers come from the inferior olive. Mossy fibers come from the pons, spinal cord and vestibular nuclei.
- **Connections:** Superior peduncle (main output to thalamus and red nucleus), middle peduncle (input from the pontine nuclei, carrying cortical information) and inferior peduncle (spinal and olivary input).
- **Functional divisions:** Vestibulocerebellum (balance and eyes), spinocerebellum (posture and limbs) and cerebrocerebellum (planning and cognition).
- **Lateralization:** Each cerebellar hemisphere controls the same side of the body (see Section 2). Cognitively, the right cerebellar hemisphere links to the left-hemisphere language cortex (crossed connections).
- **Visibility:** Posterior, inferior and lateral views. It is visible in the midsagittal cut (vermis, "arbor vitae").
- **Blood supply:** Superior cerebellar artery (SCA), anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) and posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA).
- **Damage:** Same-side ataxia, dysmetria (overshooting), intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia (clumsy rapid alternating movements), nystagmus, scanning dysarthria and wide-based gait (midline lesions). There is also CCAS. Swelling after a cerebellar stroke can compress the brainstem.
- **Kid fact:** Your cerebellum is small but holds about four out of every five of your brain's neurons. That is a lot of helpers for smooth moves and quick thinking.
- **Myths:** "The cerebellum only does balance." It also does learning, timing, language and emotion.
- **Pronunciation:** cerebellum sair-uh-BELL-um; Purkinje pur-KIN-jee; vermis VER-mis; arbor vitae AR-bor VY-tee; dysdiadochokinesia dis-dy-ad-oh-koh-kih-NEE-zhuh; Schmahmann SHMAH-mun.
- **Sources:** Azevedo et al. 2009; Schmahmann and Sherman 1998, Brain; Buckner et al. 2011, J Neurophysiol; Kandel.

### 20. Brainstem: pons and medulla oblongata
- **What:** The pons is the "bridge" bulge in front of the cerebellum. The medulla is the lowest part, continuous with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum.
- **Pons:** The basis pontis holds the pontine nuclei that relay cortex to cerebellum, plus corticospinal fibers. The tegmentum holds nuclei of CN V to VIII, the locus coeruleus (norepinephrine), raphe nuclei (serotonin), the pontine respiratory group, REM sleep generators and the medial longitudinal fasciculus.
- **Medulla:** The pyramids carry corticospinal fibers and the pyramidal decussation is here. The olives hold the inferior olivary nucleus. Nuclei of CN IX, X and XII, the nucleus of the solitary tract, the dorsal column nuclei, and cardiovascular and respiratory centers (the pre-Bötzinger complex sets the breathing rhythm; Smith et al. 1991, Science) are also here.
- **Controls:** Breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing, coughing, vomiting, sleep and arousal (reticular activating system), and facial sensation and movement.
- **Visibility:** Inferior and anterior views (basilar pons, pyramids, olives), midsagittal medial view, and lateral view below the temporal lobe.
- **Blood supply:** Pons: basilar artery (paramedian and circumferential branches) and AICA. Medulla: vertebral arteries, PICA and the anterior spinal artery.
- **Damage:** Locked-in syndrome from ventral pontine (basilar) infarcts: awake, aware, communicating with vertical eye movements and blinks. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (MLF damage). Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome from vertebral or PICA occlusion: same-side facial pain and temperature loss, opposite-side body pain and temperature loss, Horner syndrome, trouble swallowing, vertigo and ataxia. Medial medullary (Dejerine) syndrome.
- **Kid fact:** Your brainstem keeps you breathing all night, even while you dream, without you ever thinking about it.
- **Pronunciation:** pons PONZ; medulla oblongata meh-DUL-uh ob-long-GAH-tuh; locus coeruleus LOH-kus seh-ROO-lee-us; Bötzinger BERT-sing-er.
- **Sources:** Blumenfeld, chapters 14 and 15; Kandel; Nolte; Smith et al. 1991, Science.

## Section 2. Pathways and Body Control

- **Corticospinal tract:** Starts in M1, premotor cortex, SMA and S1. Runs through the corona radiata, the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the cerebral peduncles, the basis pontis and the medullary pyramids. At the pyramidal decussation (lower medulla), about 85 to 90% of fibers cross to form the lateral corticospinal tract. The remaining 10 to 15% descend uncrossed in the anterior corticospinal tract, which mostly controls trunk muscles and often crosses at spinal levels. Each pyramid carries about 1 million axons.
- **Dorsal column-medial lemniscus (fine touch, vibration, proprioception):** Receptor, then the dorsal root ganglion, then up the same-side dorsal columns (fasciculus gracilis for legs, cuneatus for arms). Synapse in the nucleus gracilis or cuneatus in the medulla, cross as internal arcuate fibers, rise in the medial lemniscus to the thalamic VPL, then reach S1.
- **Spinothalamic tract (pain, temperature, crude touch):** Synapses in the dorsal horn and crosses within one or two spinal segments in the anterior white commissure. Rises on the opposite side to the VPL, then reaches S1, the insula and the cingulate.
- **Brown-Séquard syndrome (spinal cord hemisection):** Below the lesion there is same-side weakness and same-side loss of vibration and position sense. There is opposite-side loss of pain and temperature, starting one or two segments below. It is a perfect teaching case for which tract crosses where.
- **Why does the left hemisphere control the right body?** This is an open question. Ramón y Cajal (1898) proposed that the optic chiasm crossing keeps a unified visual map, and that the motor and sensory crossings followed. The "axial twist" hypothesis (de Lussanet and Osse 2012) suggests early vertebrate embryos rotated head against body. Say that no explanation is settled.
- **Cerebellum is ipsilateral:** Cerebellar output crosses once (the superior cerebellar peduncle decussation to the opposite thalamus and motor cortex). The corticospinal tract then crosses back. Two crossings mean each cerebellar hemisphere controls the same side of the body.
- **Visual pathway:** Retinal ganglion cells form the optic nerve (about 1 to 1.2 million axons each). At the optic chiasm, fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross (about 53% of fibers in humans). Fibers from the temporal halves stay on the same side. Result: the left visual field goes to the right hemisphere. The optic tract leads to the LGN, then to the optic radiations. Meyer's loop (temporal lobe) carries the upper field, and parietal fibers carry the lower field. Then V1. Classic deficits: a chiasm lesion, such as a pituitary tumor, causes bitemporal hemianopia ("tunnel" side loss). A temporal lobe lesion causes upper quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky"). An occipital lesion causes homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.
- **Auditory pathway:** Cochlea, then CN VIII, then the cochlear nuclei, then the superior olivary complex (first point where both ears combine; used for locating sound). Then the lateral lemniscus, the inferior colliculus, the MGN, and A1. Because of bilateral representation, a stroke on one side does not cause deafness in one ear. One-ear deafness points to the ear, the nerve or the cochlear nucleus.
- **Sources:** Kandel; Purves; Blumenfeld, chapters 6, 7 and 11; Nolte.

## Section 3. The Homunculus

- **Origins:** Penfield and Boldrey 1937 (Brain) used electrical stimulation in awake epilepsy surgery patients to map motor and sensory responses. Penfield and Rasmussen 1950 (The Cerebral Cortex of Man) published the famous cartoon homunculus, drawn by the artist Hortense Cantlie.
- **Motor versus sensory:** The motor homunculus sits on the precentral gyrus (M1). The sensory homunculus sits on the postcentral gyrus (S1). Both run leg-medial to face-lateral. The sensory map includes the genitals (medial, below the foot) and the internal organs and tongue (lateral, near the insula).
- **Why are hands, lips and tongue huge?** Cortical area scales with receptor density and the need for fine control, not with body size. Fingertips have many more mechanoreceptors per square centimeter than the back.
- **The "missing female homunculus":** Classic maps were drawn mostly from male patients. Di Noto et al. 2013 (Cerebral Cortex), "The hermunculus," reviewed how little is known about female body representation, such as breast and genital mapping. Later fMRI studies have mapped female genital representation in S1. In 2021, artist Haven Wright and Preston Foerder of the University of Tennessee at Chattanooga presented what they describe as the first sculpted 3D female somatosensory homunculus ("The Missing Female Homunculus," Leonardo 54(6):653-656, December 22, 2021).
- **2023 revision (Gordon et al., Nature 617:351-359):** Precision fMRI in adults, children and infants, plus macaques, showed foot, hand and mouth regions alternating with three inter-effector regions.\[13\] These regions connect strongly to the cingulo-opercular action network and become active during planning and whole-body movement. The authors named this the somato-cognitive action network (SCAN). Status as of 2026: replicated in follow-up work by the same group and others, but criticized (Diedrichsen lab open review) on how the boundaries are interpreted.\[14\] Teach it as "a new and exciting proposal."
- **Phantom limbs and remapping:** Most amputees experience phantom sensations. Pons et al. 1991 (Science) showed large-scale remapping of S1 in monkeys after deafferentation. Ramachandran and colleagues (1990s) found that touching the face could evoke sensations in a phantom hand, consistent with the face map invading the hand area next to it. Mirror-box therapy (Ramachandran and Rogers-Ramachandran 1996) showed promise for phantom pain, but trial quality is mixed. Newer data (Makin and colleagues, for example Schone et al. 2025, Nature Neuroscience, a longitudinal study) suggest the hand map is largely preserved after amputation. That challenges the classic "invasion" story. Flag this as an active debate.
- **Sources:** Penfield and Boldrey 1937; Gordon et al. 2023, Nature; Di Noto et al. 2013, Cerebral Cortex; Kandel.

## Section 4. Function Index

P = PRIMARY, C = CONTRIBUTING. "Distributed" means no single region owns the function.

- **Working memory:** P: DLPFC, posterior parietal cortex. C: basal ganglia, cerebellum. Distributed. "Activity-silent" models are debated.
- **Episodic memory:** P: hippocampus, entorhinal cortex. C: PFC, precuneus, angular gyrus, PCC, mammillary bodies, anterior thalamus.
- **Semantic memory:** P: anterior temporal lobes (hub-and-spoke model, Lambon Ralph 2017). C: angular gyrus, MTG, inferior frontal gyrus. Debated.
- **Procedural memory:** P: basal ganglia (striatum), cerebellum. C: SMA, premotor cortex, M1.
- **Language production:** P: left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca), left posterior temporal cortex. C: SMA, insula, M1 face area, cerebellum.
- **Language comprehension:** P: left STG/STS and MTG. C: angular gyrus, IFG, anterior temporal lobe. Bilateral for some parts.
- **Reading:** P: left VWFA (fusiform). C: angular gyrus, STG, IFG.
- **Writing:** P: left superior parietal and premotor cortex (Exner's area, debated), angular gyrus. C: cerebellum, VWFA.
- **Emotion and fear:** P: amygdala. C: insula, ACC, vmPFC, hypothalamus, PAG. Emotions are network states, not single sites (debate between "basic emotion" and constructionist views; Barrett).
- **Decision-making:** P: vmPFC and OFC, striatum. C: DLPFC, ACC, insula, dopamine midbrain.
- **Attention:** P: dorsal attention network (IPS/SPL, FEF), ventral attention network (right TPJ, VFC). C: pulvinar, superior colliculus.
- **Movement:** P: M1, premotor cortex, SMA. C: basal ganglia, cerebellum, brainstem, spinal cord.
- **Balance:** P: cerebellum (vestibulocerebellum), vestibular nuclei. C: parietoinsular vestibular cortex, proprioceptive pathways.
- **Vision:** P: V1. C: V2 to V5, fusiform, parietal cortex, LGN, superior colliculus.
- **Hearing:** P: A1 (Heschl's gyrus). C: STG, MGN, inferior colliculus.
- **Touch:** P: S1. C: S2, posterior parietal cortex, VPL thalamus.
- **Pain:** No single "pain center." C: S1, S2, posterior insula, ACC, thalamus, PAG (modulation). Distributed (the "pain matrix" concept is contested; Iannetti and Mouraux 2010).
- **Smell:** P: piriform cortex. C: OFC, amygdala, entorhinal cortex.
- **Taste:** P: anterior insula and frontal operculum (gustatory cortex). C: OFC, amygdala, nucleus of the solitary tract.
- **Face recognition:** P: right FFA. C: occipital face area, posterior STS (expressions), anterior temporal lobe (identity), amygdala.
- **Spatial navigation:** P: hippocampus (place cells), entorhinal cortex (grid cells). C: retrosplenial cortex, parahippocampal place area, parietal cortex.
- **Planning:** P: DLPFC, frontopolar cortex. C: premotor cortex, basal ganglia, cerebellum.
- **Personality:** Distributed. C: vmPFC and OFC, PFC, temporal lobes, amygdala. Frontotemporal dementia shows its fragility.
- **Social cognition (including sarcasm):** P: TPJ, mPFC, STS. C: temporal pole, amygdala, right hemisphere prosody network, insula.
- **Music:** Distributed and bilateral. C: STG (right for pitch), A1, IFG, motor areas, basal ganglia (beat), cerebellum, reward circuits (nucleus accumbens). Music-selective neural populations have been reported in auditory cortex (Norman-Haignere et al. 2015).
- **Math and number:** P: intraparietal sulcus (quantity; Dehaene's triple-code model). C: angular gyrus (math facts), PFC, fusiform (digit form).
- **Sleep and wakefulness:** P: hypothalamus (VLPO for sleep, orexin/hypocretin neurons for wake), brainstem reticular activating system. C: thalamus, basal forebrain, pineal (melatonin), suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian clock).
- **Hunger and thirst:** P: hypothalamus (arcuate, lateral, paraventricular nuclei; lamina terminalis/OVLT for thirst). C: brainstem NTS, insula, OFC.
- **Body temperature:** P: hypothalamus (preoptic area). C: brainstem, autonomic outflow.
- **Breathing and heart rate:** P: medulla (pre-Bötzinger complex, NTS, ventrolateral medulla), pons. C: hypothalamus, insula, ACC.
- **Consciousness:** Debated. Arousal needs the brainstem reticular formation, the thalamus (intralaminar nuclei) and cortex. Theories of content (global workspace, integrated information) disagree on whether front or back cortex matters most. A 2025 adversarial collaboration (Cogitate Consortium, Nature) challenged parts of both theories.

## Section 5. Hidden and Deep Structures

- **Insula:** Cortex hidden deep in the lateral fissure, covered by the opercula. It handles interoception (body-state awareness), taste (anterior insula and frontal operculum), pain, disgust, emotional awareness and salience (anterior insula). Supplied by the MCA (M2 branches). Damage can cause taste disturbance, apraxia of speech (debated) and reduced craving in smokers (Naqvi et al. 2007, Science). Pronunciation IN-suh-luh.
- **Thalamus:** A paired gray-matter relay and gatekeeper above the midbrain. Relay nuclei: LGN (vision), MGN (hearing), VPL (body touch and pain), VPM (face and taste), VA/VL (motor loops from basal ganglia and cerebellum), anterior nucleus (memory, Papez circuit), mediodorsal nucleus (PFC), pulvinar (attention and visual association). The reticular nucleus controls the gating. Every sense except smell relays here before reaching primary cortex. Supplied by PCA branches (thalamoperforators, thalamogeniculate) and the posterior communicating artery. Damage can cause Dejerine-Roussy thalamic pain syndrome.
- **Hypothalamus:** Below the thalamus, about 4 g. It controls homeostasis (temperature, hunger, thirst, circadian rhythm via the SCN, sleep), drives the pituitary, and directs autonomic and defensive behavior.
- **Pituitary gland:** About pea-sized, sitting in the sella turcica. Anterior lobe (adenohypophysis): growth hormone, prolactin, ACTH, TSH, LH and FSH, controlled by hypothalamic releasing hormones through the portal circulation. Posterior lobe (neurohypophysis): oxytocin and vasopressin made in hypothalamic neurons. It sits just below the optic chiasm, which is why pituitary tumors cause bitemporal vision loss.
- **Basal ganglia:** Caudate and putamen (together, the striatum), globus pallidus (external and internal), subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra. Direct pathway: cortex, then striatum (D1), then GPi/SNr inhibited, then thalamus released, then movement facilitated. Indirect pathway: striatum (D2), then GPe, then STN, then GPi, then thalamus suppressed. Dopamine boosts direct and suppresses indirect pathway activity. The model (Albin, Young and Penney 1989; DeLong 1990) is a useful simplification, and newer work shows both pathways co-activate. Loss of dopamine causes Parkinsonism. Huntington's disease starts in the caudate. The subthalamic nucleus and GPi are deep brain stimulation targets.
- **Hippocampus:** A curled structure in the medial temporal lobe ("seahorse"). It forms new episodic memories and supports spatial navigation. Place cells were discovered by John O'Keefe (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky 1971). Grid cells in the entorhinal cortex were discovered by May-Britt and Edvard Moser (Hafting et al. 2005, Nature). All three shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Patient H.M. (Henry Molaison, 1926-2008) had both medial temporal lobes removed in 1953 by William Scoville to treat epilepsy. He lost the ability to form new declarative memories but could learn motor skills, such as mirror drawing (Scoville and Milner 1957). Supplied by the PCA and the anterior choroidal artery. Adult neurogenesis is debated (see Section 12).
- **Amygdala:** An almond-shaped cluster of nuclei in front of the hippocampus. It detects threat and salience, supports fear learning and emotional memory, and processes social signals such as eye gaze. Patient S.M. (Urbach-Wiethe disease, bilateral amygdala damage) showed impaired fear experience (Feinstein et al. 2011, Current Biology). It is not "the fear center" alone: it also responds to reward and novelty.
- **Corpus callosum (brief):** About 200 million axons (estimates vary; Tomasch 1954 gives about 200 million) linking the two hemispheres. Split-brain studies by Sperry and Gazzaniga are covered in Section 10.
- **Pineal gland:** A small midline gland behind the third ventricle that secretes melatonin at night under SCN control. It often calcifies with age, making it a useful X-ray landmark. Descartes called it the seat of the soul (historical, not science).
- **Sources:** Kandel; Purves; Blumenfeld, chapters 16 to 18; Scoville and Milner 1957, J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry; Nobel Assembly 2014 press release.

## Section 7. Meninges, Ventricles and CSF

- **Meninges:** Dura mater (tough outer layer; forms the falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli and venous sinuses), arachnoid mater (web-like; the subarachnoid space beneath it holds CSF and major arteries) and pia mater (delicate, follows every gyrus). The brain tissue itself has no pain receptors. The dura and blood vessels do, which is why headaches are possible.
- **Ventricles:** Two lateral ventricles connect through the interventricular foramina (of Monro) to the third ventricle. The cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius) links it to the fourth ventricle, which drains through the median aperture (Magendie) and lateral apertures (Luschka) into the subarachnoid space.
- **CSF:** Total volume about 125 to 150 mL in adults, with about 25 mL in the ventricles. Production is about 500 mL per day (estimates range from about 400 to 600 mL), so CSF turns over roughly three to four times a day. The choroid plexus makes most of it, but the exact share and extra-choroidal sources are debated. It is absorbed through arachnoid granulations into the venous sinuses, and also through perineural and meningeal lymphatic routes (meningeal lymphatics rediscovered by Louveau et al. 2015, Nature, and Aspelund et al. 2015, J Exp Med). Functions: buoyancy (a 1,400 g brain weighs only about 25 to 50 g when floating), cushioning, chemical stability and waste removal.
- **Hydrocephalus:** Too much CSF, from blockage (obstructive, for example aqueductal stenosis), poor absorption (communicating) or, rarely, overproduction. Normal pressure hydrocephalus in older adults causes the triad of gait trouble, urinary incontinence and cognitive decline. Informational only.
- **Glymphatic system (DEBATED):** Iliff et al. 2012 (Science Translational Medicine) proposed that CSF flows along spaces around arteries, through brain tissue with the help of aquaporin-4 water channels on astrocytes, and out along veins. Xie et al. 2013 (Science) reported faster clearance during sleep.\[18\] Miao et al. 2024 (Nature Neuroscience, Franks and Wisden lab) found clearance reduced during sleep and anesthesia, in male mice.\[22\] Script line: "Scientists agree the brain clears waste. They are still arguing about how, and whether sleep speeds it up."
- **Sources:** Purves; Nolte; StatPearls "Physiology, Cerebral Spinal Fluid"; Xie et al. 2013; Miao et al. 2024.

## Section 8. Sensory Systems End to End

- **Vision:** About 90 to 120 million rods and about 6 million cones per retina. Photoreceptors pass signals to bipolar cells, then to retinal ganglion cells (about 1 to 1.2 million axons). Then the optic nerve, chiasm and tract, the LGN (six layers: magnocellular, parvocellular, plus koniocellular), the optic radiations, V1, then the dorsal and ventral streams. A side route goes to the superior colliculus (orienting) and the SCN (circadian light). Notable: the blind spot, where the optic disc has no photoreceptors, is filled in by the brain.
- **Hearing:** Sound vibrates the eardrum and the ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes, the smallest bones in the body), then the cochlea. The basilar membrane separates sound by frequency (tonotopy). About 3,500 inner hair cells carry most of the signal, and about 12,000 outer hair cells amplify it. Then CN VIII, the cochlear nuclei, the superior olive, the inferior colliculus, the MGN, A1 and the STG. Human range is about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, and the upper limit drops with age.
- **Touch, proprioception and pain:** Receptors: Meissner corpuscles (light flutter), Merkel cells (pressure and edges), Pacinian corpuscles (vibration), Ruffini endings (stretch), and muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs (proprioception). These use the DCML pathway to VPL, then S1, S2 and parietal cortex. Pain and temperature use free nerve endings (fast Aδ fibers and slow C fibers), the spinothalamic tract, VPL and many cortical sites, with descending control from the PAG. Molecular sensors: TRPV1 (heat and capsaicin) and Piezo2 (touch and proprioception). These earned David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian the 2021 Nobel Prize. The 2026 Brain Prize went to David Ginty and Patrik Ernfors for mapping the cell types of touch and pain.
- **Smell:** Odorants bind receptors on olfactory sensory neurons in the nasal epithelium. Humans have about 400 functional olfactory receptor genes (Buck and Axel 1991, Cell; Nobel 2004). Each sensory neuron expresses one receptor type. These neurons regenerate throughout life. Their axons pass through the cribriform plate to glomeruli in the olfactory bulb, then mitral and tufted cells carry signals along the lateral olfactory tract to the piriform cortex, amygdala and entorhinal cortex, with no thalamic relay first. The OFC is reached directly and via the mediodorsal thalamus. **Disputed claim:** "Humans can discriminate 1 trillion odors" (Bushdid et al. 2014, Science) was challenged by Meister 2015 (eLife) and Gerkin and Castro 2015 (eLife), who showed the method could produce almost any number.\[23\]\[24\]\[25\] Say "the true number is unknown, but it is large."
- **Taste:** Five well-established basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Fat ("oleogustus") and others are candidates. Taste receptor cells sit in taste buds. Signals travel via CN VII (front two-thirds of tongue), IX (back third) and X (throat), to the nucleus of the solitary tract, then VPM thalamus, then the gustatory cortex in the anterior insula and frontal operculum, then the OFC (flavor, with smell). **Myth:** the "tongue map" of zones for each taste comes from a misreading of Hänig (1901) and was popularized by Boring (1942). All tastes are detected across the tongue.
- **Sources:** Kandel (sensory chapters); Purves; Meister 2015; Gerkin and Castro 2015; Nobel Assembly 2004 and 2021.

## Section 9. Brain Networks

- **Default mode network (DMN):** Members are the posterior cingulate and precuneus, medial PFC, angular gyrus and inferior parietal lobule, lateral temporal cortex, and hippocampal formation. It is active during rest, mind-wandering, remembering, imagining the future and thinking about others. History: Shulman et al. 1997 noticed consistent task-induced deactivations. Raichle et al. 2001 (PNAS), "A default mode of brain function," named it. Greicius et al. 2003 (PNAS) showed resting functional connectivity. Buckner, Andrews-Hanna and Schacter 2008 is the key review. Resting-state connectivity itself was first shown by Biswal et al. 1995 in the motor cortex.
- **Salience network:** Anterior insula and dorsal ACC, plus amygdala and ventral striatum. It detects what matters and switches between the DMN and executive networks. Seeley et al. 2007 (J Neurosci); Menon and Uddin 2010.
- **Central executive (frontoparietal control) network:** DLPFC and posterior parietal cortex (IPS). It supports working memory, flexible control and rule use. Seeley et al. 2007; Vincent et al. 2008; Yeo et al. 2011's 7-network atlas.
- **Dorsal attention network:** IPS/SPL and FEF, bilateral. It handles goal-driven (top-down) attention. **Ventral attention network:** right TPJ and ventral frontal cortex. It reorients attention to unexpected stimuli. Corbetta and Shulman 2002 (Nat Rev Neurosci). Damage to the ventral network is linked to neglect.
- **Language network:** Left-lateralized frontal (IFG, MFG) and temporal (STG, MTG, anterior temporal) regions, identified with functional localizers in individual brains (Fedorenko et al. 2010, J Neurophysiol). It is selective for language and largely not engaged by math, music or logic (Fedorenko, Behr and Kanwisher 2011, PNAS). Fedorenko, Piantadosi and Gibson 2024 (Nature) argued that language is mainly a tool for communication rather than for thought. That is a position paper, and contested.
- **Caveat:** Network boundaries depend on the atlas used, such as Yeo 7 or 17 networks or the Gordon parcellation. Individual brains differ from group maps (precision functional mapping).

## Section 10. History and Originators

- **Edwin Smith Papyrus:** An Egyptian surgical text copied around 1600 BCE from older sources. It describes 48 injury cases and contains the earliest known written word for "brain," including links between head injury and paralysis.
- **Hippocrates (around 460-370 BCE):** "On the Sacred Disease" argued that epilepsy is a brain disorder, not divine, and that the brain is the seat of thought and feeling.
- **Aristotle (384-322 BCE):** Held a cardiocentric view. The heart was the seat of mind, and the brain cooled the blood.
- **Galen (around 129-216 CE):** Experiments on animals. He cut the recurrent laryngeal nerve in pigs, which silenced their squeals. Promoted ventricular "animal spirits." His influence lasted about 1,400 years.
- **Andreas Vesalius:** De humani corporis fabrica (1543) corrected many of Galen's errors through human dissection.
- **René Descartes:** Mechanistic reflexes. He placed the soul's interface in the pineal gland (Treatise of Man, published 1662/1664).
- **Thomas Willis:** Cerebri Anatome (1664), illustrated by Christopher Wren. He described the arterial circle at the brain's base (the circle of Willis) and coined the term "neurology" (neurologie).
- **Franciscus Sylvius (Franz de le Boë, 1614-1672):** The lateral sulcus is called the Sylvian fissure in his honor. Caspar Bartholin published the description in 1641 and credited Sylvius. The cerebral aqueduct is also called the aqueduct of Sylvius. Note that a different, earlier Jacobus Sylvius (1478-1555) is sometimes confused with him.
- **Luigi Rolando (1773-1831):** Italian anatomist. The central sulcus is the "fissure of Rolando." Félix Vicq d'Azyr depicted it earlier, and François Leuret named it after Rolando in 1839. **Game note:** Sylvi is named for the Sylvian (lateral) fissure, which separates the temporal lobe from the frontal and parietal lobes. Rollo is named for the Rolandic (central) fissure, which separates the frontal and parietal lobes and has motor cortex in front and sensory cortex behind.
- **Franz Joseph Gall (1758-1828) and phrenology:** Gall proposed that mental faculties are localized in the cortex, a correct general idea. He claimed that skull bumps reveal faculty size, which was wrong. Skull shape does not mirror the cortex beneath it, the "faculties" were made up, and the evidence was anecdotal and confirmation-biased.
- **Pierre Flourens (1794-1867):** Ablation experiments in birds. He showed the cerebellum's role in coordination and the medulla's role in breathing. He argued the cortex works as a whole (equipotentiality), opposing phrenology.
- **Paul Broca (1824-1880):** Leborgne ("Tan") in 1861. Left-hemisphere language dominance in 1865.
- **Carl Wernicke (1848-1905):** Sensory aphasia in 1874, and the connectionist model of language.
- **John Hughlings Jackson (1835-1911):** The "Jacksonian march" of focal seizures, which suggested a cortical somatotopic map. Also proposed hierarchical levels of nervous system organization.
- **Camillo Golgi (1843-1926):** The silver chromate "black reaction" stain, around 1873. Supported the reticular theory, that the nervous system is one continuous net.
- **Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1852-1934):** Used Golgi's stain to argue that neurons are separate cells (the neuron doctrine) and proposed dynamic polarization. Shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Golgi, despite their opposing views.
- **Korbinian Brodmann (1868-1918):** Cytoarchitectonic map (1909), with areas numbered up to 52. Not all are present in humans.
- **Charles Sherrington (1857-1952):** Coined "synapse" in 1897. Studied reflexes and integration. Nobel 1932, with Edgar Adrian.
- **Otto Loewi (1873-1961):** The 1921 frog heart experiment showed chemical neurotransmission ("Vagusstoff," later identified as acetylcholine). Nobel 1936, with Henry Dale.
- **Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley:** Squid giant axon experiments and the 1952 model of the action potential (sodium and potassium currents). Nobel 1963, with John Eccles.
- **Wilder Penfield (1891-1976):** Montreal Neurological Institute. Awake brain stimulation, the homunculus and the Montreal procedure for epilepsy surgery.
- **Brenda Milner (born 1918, died 2025):** Studied H.M. and founded neuropsychology of memory. She showed that motor learning was spared in H.M. Her death was reported in 2025 at age 106. Verify the date before scripting.
- **Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga:** Split-brain studies from the 1960s on patients with the corpus callosum cut. Each hemisphere could process information separately. The left was dominant for speech, and the right was better at some visuospatial tasks. Sperry won the Nobel in 1981. These studies were later over-popularized into the "left brain/right brain" personality myth.
- **Phineas Gage (1823-1860):** On September 13, 1848, a tamping iron passed through his left frontal lobe. His doctor, John Harlow, reported major personality change. Macmillan and Lena 2010 (Neuropsychological Rehabilitation) documented that Gage later worked for years as a stagecoach driver in Chile on the Valparaíso-Santiago route, a job demanding planning and social skill. A physician there reported no impairment of his mental faculties. This suggests substantial psychosocial recovery. Daguerreotype portraits identified in 2009 show him well-dressed and composed.
- **Henry Molaison (H.M.):** See Section 5. His brain was sliced into 2,401 sections and digitally mapped (Annese et al. 2014, Nature Communications).
- **Sources:** Kandel (history chapters); Finger, Origins of Neuroscience (academic history); Macmillan and Lena 2010; Nobel Prize official site.

## Section 11. Modern Research and Leaders (date-stamped October 2026)

- **Human Connectome Project (launched 2010, NIH):** Mapped about 1,200 young adults with high-quality MRI. Glasser et al. 2016 (Nature) produced a map of 180 areas per hemisphere (HCP-MMP1.0). ESTABLISHED.
- **FlyWire (October 2, 2024, Nature):** The complete adult female fruit fly brain connectome, with 139,255 neurons and about 54.5 million synapses, published in a nine-paper Nature package (Dorkenwald et al. 2024; Schlegel et al. 2024). The consortium was co-led by Sebastian Seung and Mala Murthy at Princeton.\[26\]\[27\] ESTABLISHED dataset.
- **MICrONS (April 9, 2025, Nature):** A cubic millimeter of mouse visual cortex with more than 200,000 cells, about 523 million synapses and functional recordings from about 75,000 neurons, from Baylor, the Allen Institute and Princeton (The MICrONS Consortium 2025, Nature 640:435-447).\[28\]\[29\]\[30\] ESTABLISHED dataset. Its interpretation is ongoing. All data come from one mouse.\[31\]
- **Human cortex fragment:** Shapson-Coe et al. 2024 (Science) reconstructed 1 mm³ of human temporal cortex (about 57,000 cells and about 150 million synapses) from Harvard and Google. A single surgical sample.
- **NIH BRAIN Initiative:** Launched April 2013. BRAIN CONNECTS (from 2023) aims at whole-mouse-brain connectivity tools. The BRAIN Initiative Cell Census Network published a large set of human and mouse cell atlas papers in 2023 (Science, Nature). Siletti et al. 2023 (Science) profiled more than 3 million human brain nuclei, revealing hundreds of cell-type clusters and thousands of subclusters. Note that US federal research funding in 2025-2026 has been unstable. Check current BRAIN Initiative budget status before stating figures.
- **Neuroimaging:** 7 tesla MRI was first FDA-cleared for clinical use in 2017. The 11.7 T Iseult scanner (CEA, France) released its first in vivo human brain images in 2024. Diffusion MRI tractography maps white-matter pathways but has known false-positive problems (Maier-Hein et al. 2017, Nature Communications). ESTABLISHED tools with known limits.
- **Brain-computer interfaces:**
  - BrainGate: Hochberg et al. 2006 (Nature) cursor control; 2012 robotic arm. Willett et al. 2023 (Nature) decoded speech at 62 words per minute.
  - UC Davis (Stavisky and Brandman): Card et al. 2024 (NEJM, August 15, 2024). A man with ALS reached 99.6% accuracy on a 50-word vocabulary on day one, 90.2% on a 125,000-word vocabulary on day two, and about 97.5% accuracy sustained over about 8 months.\[32\]\[33\]\[34\] A single participant, early-stage.
  - UCSF (Edward Chang): Metzger et al. 2023 (Nature) decoded speech plus a digital avatar at about 78 words per minute. A 2025 Nature Neuroscience streaming brain-to-voice study followed (Littlejohn et al., with Anumanchipalli at UC Berkeley). Single or few participants.
  - Neuralink: First human implant in January 2024. The company reported 21 participants on January 28, 2026. According to an August 2026 secondary review, no peer-reviewed human data had been published and no Blindsight trial was registered.\[5\] Blindsight received FDA Breakthrough Device designation in September 2024.\[35\] EARLY-STAGE; company claims.
  - Synchron: Stentrode placed through the jugular vein into a vessel next to motor cortex. Small early feasibility studies (COMMAND, US). EARLY-STAGE.
  - Precision Neuroscience: Thin-film surface electrode array. On April 17, 2025, the company announced FDA 510(k) clearance for its Layer 7 Cortical Interface (1,024 electrodes) for recording, monitoring and stimulating electrical activity on the brain's surface, with implantation of up to 30 days. EARLY-STAGE.
- **Optogenetics:** Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel and Deisseroth 2005 (Nature Neuroscience) showed light control of neurons with channelrhodopsin-2. Brain Prize 2013 (shared by six researchers, including Boyden and Deisseroth); Lasker 2021 (Deisseroth, Hegemann, Oesterhelt). ESTABLISHED as a research tool. First human vision restoration case reported by Sahel et al. 2021 (Nature Medicine), a single patient.
- **Prizes (latest):** Kavli Prize in Neuroscience 2026: Christine Holt, Kelsey Martin, Erin Schuman and Oswald Steward, for local protein translation in neurons (announced June 10, 2026).\[36\]\[37\] Brain Prize 2026: David Ginty and Patrik Ernfors (touch and pain).\[38\] Brain Prize 2025: Michelle Monje and Frank Winkler (cancer neuroscience).\[39\] The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine had not been announced as of October 3, 2026. Update after the announcement.

## Section 12. Lifespan Development

- **Neural tube:** Neurulation begins around day 18 to 21 after conception. The tube closes in weeks 3 to 4. The anterior neuropore closes around day 25, the posterior around day 27 to 28. Failure causes anencephaly or spina bifida. Folic acid before and in early pregnancy lowers the risk (CDC). Informational only.
- **Neurogenesis and migration:** Most cortical neurons are born between about weeks 5 and 20 to 25 of gestation. They migrate along radial glia (inside-out layering: deep layers first). Outer radial glia expanded greatly in primates.
- **Gyrification:** The lateral fissure appears first (around week 14 to 16). The central and calcarine sulci appear around weeks 16 to 20 or more. Most secondary and tertiary folding happens in the third trimester. The full-term brain shows the adult folding pattern. Preterm brains are smoother.
- **Synaptogenesis and pruning:** Synapse density peaks earlier in sensory cortex (visual cortex in the first year) and later in the PFC. Pruning continues through adolescence (Huttenlocher and Dabholkar 1997, J Comp Neurol).
- **Myelination:** Begins prenatally and is rapid in the first two years. It continues into the third decade, with the frontal and association tracts last. Bethlehem et al. 2022 (Nature, "Brain charts for the human lifespan," more than 100,000 scans) estimated cortical gray matter volume peaks at about age 6 and white matter volume at about age 29.
- **Critical periods:** Hubel and Wiesel showed that depriving one eye early in life permanently changes ocular dominance in kittens. In humans, untreated childhood amblyopia and congenital cataracts show visual critical periods. Language has sensitive periods, especially for accent and grammar. These are "sensitive," not absolute.
- **Adolescence and the PFC:** Gogtay et al. 2004 (PNAS, ages 4 to 21) showed higher-order association cortex matures last.\[15\] "Fully mature at 25" is an extrapolation beyond that data. Mousley et al. 2025 (Nature Communications) found the strongest whole-brain topological turning point at about 32.\[16\] **Resolution:** "Prefrontal and whole-brain wiring keep maturing through the twenties, and some network measures change until about the early thirties. There is no single 'done' birthday" (see also Somerville 2016, Neuron).\[40\]
- **2025 lifespan "eras" study (SINGLE STUDY):** Mousley, Bethlehem, Yeh and Astle, Nature Communications 16:10055, November 25, 2025. Diffusion MRI from 4,216 people aged 0 to 90 found turning points at about 9, 32, 66 and 83 years, defining five epochs.\[16\]\[41\]\[42\] It measures structural network topology. Media framing of "adolescence lasts until 32" overstates it.\[43\]
- **Normal aging:** Total brain volume declines slowly from midlife, faster after about 60 (often quoted at about 0.2 to 0.5% per year, rising with age; varies by study). Processing speed, episodic memory and some executive functions decline on average. Vocabulary, general knowledge (crystallized intelligence) and emotional regulation often hold or improve. People vary widely.
- **Dementia versus normal aging:** Dementia is a decline that interferes with independent daily life, caused by disease (Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal). It is not an inevitable part of aging. Informational only.
- **Cognitive reserve:** People with more education, more complex jobs and more social engagement tolerate more brain pathology before symptoms appear (Stern 2002 and 2012). The Lancet Commission (Livingston et al. 2024) estimated that about 45% of dementia cases are potentially linked to 14 modifiable risk factors. This is a population estimate, not individual advice.
- **Adult neurogenesis (DEBATED):** Eriksson et al. 1998 (Nature Medicine) and Spalding et al. 2013 (Cell, carbon-14 dating) supported it.\[44\] Sorrells et al. 2018 (Nature) found it undetectable in adults. Boldrini et al. 2018 (Cell Stem Cell) and Moreno-Jiménez et al. 2019 (Nature Medicine) reported it.\[44\]\[45\] Dumitru et al. 2025 (Science, Frisén lab) identified dividing neural progenitors in adults aged up to 78.\[46\] Only 354 progenitor cells were found among about 300,000 nuclei, numbers varied widely, and several adults showed none.\[46\]\[47\]\[48\] Script line: "Evidence is growing that adults make some new neurons in the hippocampus, but how many, and whether it matters for memory, is still debated."

## Section 13. Blood Supply

- **Inflow:** Two internal carotid arteries (anterior circulation) and two vertebral arteries, which join to form the basilar artery (posterior circulation).
- **Circle of Willis:** Anterior communicating artery, both ACAs, internal carotid terminals, posterior communicating arteries and both PCAs. It allows backup flow between circulations. A complete "textbook" circle exists in only a minority of people (often cited at under half; estimates vary).
- **Territories and game regions:**
  - ACA (medial frontal and parietal): medial PFC, medial OFC, SMA, leg areas of M1 and S1, anterior cingulate, precuneus (with PCA).
  - MCA (lateral convexity): lateral PFC, lateral OFC, premotor cortex, M1 and S1 for face and arm, Broca's area, SPL (with border zones), angular gyrus, A1, STG, Wernicke's area, insula, piriform cortex (with the anterior choroidal artery).
  - PCA (occipital and inferior temporal): V1, visual association cortex, fusiform gyrus, posterior cingulate (with ACA), thalamus, hippocampus (with the anterior choroidal artery), midbrain branches.
  - Vertebrobasilar system: midbrain (basilar tip, SCA, PCA perforators), pons (basilar), medulla (vertebral, PICA, anterior spinal), cerebellum (SCA, AICA, PICA).
- **Venous drainage:** Superficial cortical veins drain to the superior sagittal sinus. Deep veins (internal cerebral veins and the basal vein of Rosenthal) drain to the great vein of Galen, then the straight sinus. Then the confluence of sinuses, the transverse and sigmoid sinuses, and the internal jugular veins.
- **Energy:** The brain is about 2% of body mass but uses about 20% of resting oxygen and energy (Raichle and Gusnard 2002, PNAS).\[49\] Most of that supports synaptic signaling and restoring ion gradients.
- **Blood-brain barrier:** Tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, supported by pericytes and astrocyte end-feet, limit what enters from blood. That is why many drugs cannot reach the brain, and why gut serotonin does not cross into the brain.
- **Sources:** Blumenfeld, chapter 10; Purves; Nolte; Raichle and Gusnard 2002.

## Section 14. Stroke and Recovery (informational only)

- **Types:** Ischemic stroke (blocked artery) is most common. Of 7.25 million stroke deaths in 2021, about 3.59 million were ischemic, 3.31 million intracerebral hemorrhage and 0.35 million subarachnoid hemorrhage (GBD 2021, via AHA 2025 Statistics Update and WSO 2025).\[2\] A transient ischemic attack (TIA) is temporary symptoms without permanent damage, and it is a warning sign.
- **BE FAST (safety information, allowed):** The American Stroke Association's B.E. F.A.S.T. stands for Balance loss, Eye (vision) changes, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911 (or local emergency services).\[50\] **Flag:** the ASA used F.A.S.T. as its official public message from 2013.\[51\] A preliminary 2025 ASA conference study (Adeoye et al., International Stroke Conference) found both acronyms prompted 911 calls equally, but FAST was easier to remember.\[52\] Script: "If you notice these signs, call emergency services right away and note the time symptoms started."
- **Time is brain:** Saver 2006 (Stroke 37:263-266). Each minute of untreated large-vessel ischemic stroke costs about 1.9 million neurons, 14 billion synapses and 12 km of myelinated fibers. Each hour ages the brain by about 3.6 years compared with normal aging. This is a modeled estimate.\[21\]
- **Symptoms by territory and hemisphere:** MCA (left): right face and arm weakness, aphasia. MCA (right): left face and arm weakness, left neglect, anosognosia. ACA: opposite leg weakness, apathy. PCA: opposite visual field loss, and memory or reading problems if on the left. Vertebrobasilar: vertigo, double vision, ataxia, crossed signs, and reduced consciousness.
- **Treatments (general, dated):** Intravenous alteplase (tPA) was FDA-approved in 1996 after the NINDS trial (1995, NEJM). Mechanical thrombectomy was proven in 2015 by five trials, including MR CLEAN (December 2014, NEJM), ESCAPE, EXTEND-IA, SWIFT PRIME and REVASCAT. DAWN and DEFUSE 3 (2018) extended selected patients to 24 hours. Tenecteplase (TNKase) received FDA approval for acute ischemic stroke on March 3, 2025, as announced by Genentech, based on the AcT trial of 1,577 patients at 22 Canadian stroke centers. Not individual advice.
- **Rehabilitation and plasticity:** Recovery is fastest in the first weeks to months, through spontaneous recovery and neural reorganization. Constraint-induced movement therapy (Taub) restricts the stronger arm to force use of the weaker one. The EXCITE trial (Wolf et al. 2006, JAMA) showed benefit. Aphasia and neglect therapies are evolving.
- **Global statistics (date-stamped, GBD 2021, published 2024-2025):** Per WHO, stroke was the third leading cause of death and disability in 2021, with 93.8 million people living after a stroke, 11.9 million new strokes, and a lifetime risk of 1 in 4 adults.\[53\] About 7.25 million deaths (WSO 2025), with about 87% of stroke deaths in lower-income and lower-middle-income countries.\[54\]\[55\] GBD 2023 updates may supersede these figures.

## Section 15. Tumors (informational only; no survival statistics in general or child content)

- **Primary versus metastatic:** Primary tumors start in the brain or its coverings. Metastatic tumors spread from elsewhere (lung, breast, melanoma). In adults, metastases are more common than primary malignant brain tumors.
- **Meningioma:** The most common primary brain and CNS tumor. It arises from arachnoid cap cells, is mostly non-malignant, and is more common in women.
- **Gliomas:** Arise from glial lineages. Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults.
- **Pediatric tumors:** Pilocytic astrocytoma (often cerebellar, usually low grade) and medulloblastoma (cerebellar, malignant, the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor; molecular groups WNT, SHH, Group 3 and Group 4).
- **WHO CNS5 (2021; Louis et al. 2021, Neuro-Oncology):** Combines microscope appearance and molecular markers. Adult diffuse gliomas fall into three groups: astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (CNS WHO grade 2, 3 or 4); oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted (grade 2 or 3); and glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (grade 4). Grades now use Arabic numerals and are assigned within each tumor type.
- **Location shapes symptoms:** Frontal tumors cause personality change and seizures. Tumors near M1 cause weakness. Temporal tumors cause seizures, language or memory problems. Occipital tumors cause visual field loss. Cerebellar tumors cause ataxia and hydrocephalus. Pituitary tumors cause bitemporal vision loss and hormonal effects. Brainstem tumors affect cranial nerves.
- **Treatment categories (general):** Surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, targeted therapy (for example, the FDA Approval Summary reports that the IDH inhibitor vorasidenib received traditional approval on August 6, 2024, for patients 12 and older with grade 2 astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma carrying an IDH1 or IDH2 mutation, the first targeted therapy for these gliomas), tumor-treating fields and clinical trials.
- **CBTRUS statistics: DO NOT SPEAK BY DEFAULT.** CBTRUS 2025 report (US, 2018-2022; Neuro-Oncology, October 14, 2025): the incidence of all primary brain and CNS tumors is 26.05 per 100,000 (malignant 6.86, non-malignant 19.19). Meningioma accounts for 42.6% of all tumors. Glioblastoma accounts for 13.7% of all tumors and 52.2% of malignant tumors. Gliomas account for 22.2%. Incidence in ages 0 to 19 is 5.99 per 100,000. Incidence is higher in women (29.67) than men (22.23).\[56\]

## Section 16. Scope Candidates (brief)

- **Alzheimer's disease:** The most common cause of dementia, with amyloid plaques, tau tangles and neuron loss starting in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus. Lecanemab (Leqembi) got accelerated FDA approval on January 6, 2023, and traditional approval on July 6, 2023.\[57\] Donanemab (Kisunla) was approved on July 2, 2024.\[58\] Both are anti-amyloid antibodies for early-stage disease that modestly slow decline, with risks of ARIA (brain swelling or bleeding seen on MRI).\[59\]\[60\] Recommendation: include at a general level, with no treatment advice.
- **Parkinson's disease:** Loss of dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, plus alpha-synuclein Lewy bodies. Motor signs (tremor, slowness, rigidity) appear after substantial loss. Older textbook estimates say 50 to 80%, while newer estimates are lower for cell bodies and higher for terminals. Deep brain stimulation targets the subthalamic nucleus or GPi. Recommendation: include. It pairs well with the midbrain region.
- **Epilepsy:** The WHO epilepsy fact sheet says around 50 million people worldwide have epilepsy, nearly 80% of them in low- and middle-income countries, and that up to 70% could live seizure-free if properly diagnosed and treated. It ties in with Penfield, H.M., split-brain studies and Jacksonian march. Recommendation: include. Seizure first aid (keep safe, time it, call for help if prolonged) counts as safety information.
- **Concussion and TBI:** A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by a blow or jolt. The OFC and anterior temporal lobes are especially vulnerable. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is diagnosed only after death, and its causal link with repeated head impacts is debated, though strengthening. Recommendation: include, with helmet safety messaging.
- **Animal brains (child-friendly):** Neuron counts by species, mostly from Herculano-Houzel's isotropic fractionator work:
  - Mouse: about 70 million neurons.
  - Rat: about 200 million.
  - Cat cerebral cortex: about 250 million. Dog cerebral cortex: about 530 million (Jardim-Messeder et al. 2017).
  - Macaque: about 6.4 billion.
  - Human: about 86 billion.
  - African elephant: about 257 billion, but about 98% are in the cerebellum, with only about 5.6 billion in the cortex (Herculano-Houzel et al. 2014).
  - Octopus: about 500 million neurons, roughly two-thirds in the arms.
  - Sperm whales have the largest brains, about 8 kg.
  - Smooth (lissencephalic) brains: mouse, rat, marmoset. Folded (gyrencephalic) brains: human, dolphin, elephant. Folding scales with cortex size and thickness, not "smartness" alone.

## Section 17. Support Material

### 17a. Consolidated myths list

1. **"We use only 10% of our brain."** False. Imaging shows activity across the whole brain over a day, and damage almost anywhere has effects (Beyerstein 1999).
2. **"Left-brained logical, right-brained creative people."** False. Nielsen et al. 2013 (PLoS ONE, more than 1,000 people) found no evidence for people being overall left- or right-brain dominant. Some functions are lateralized (language left, face and prosody right-biased), but both hemispheres work together.
3. **"Learning styles: teach to visual or auditory learners."** Not supported (Pashler et al. 2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest).
4. **"Bigger brain means smarter."** The correlation is weak to modest (meta-analysis r of about 0.24; Pietschnig et al. 2015). Elephants and whales have larger brains than humans.
5. **"Mozart effect: classical music makes babies smarter."** The original effect (Rauscher et al. 1993, Nature) was a short-lived boost in spatial task scores in college students. Meta-analyses found little or no effect (Chabris 1999; Pietschnig et al. 2010).
6. **"Alcohol kills brain cells."** Too simple. Studies found no major loss of neocortical neurons in people with alcoholism (Jensen and Pakkenberg 1993, Lancet), but alcohol damages white matter and dendrites. Thiamine deficiency (Wernicke-Korsakoff) does kill neurons. Heavy use harms the brain.
7. **"100 billion neurons."** Use about 86 billion (Azevedo et al. 2009).
8. **"Glia outnumber neurons 10 to 1."** About 1:1 overall (Azevedo et al. 2009; von Bartheld et al. 2016).
9. **"The cerebellum only controls balance."** It also handles learning, timing, cognition and emotion (Schmahmann).
10. **"Smell goes through the thalamus like other senses."** Smell reaches the piriform cortex directly. A secondary route through the thalamus to the OFC exists.
11. **"Adults never grow new neurons" or "adults definitely grow many new neurons."** Both overstate the evidence. It is still debated (Section 12).
12. **"Phineas Gage became a permanent wreck."** He showed substantial recovery (Macmillan and Lena 2010).
13. **"Humans can smell a trillion odors."** The claim is disputed (Meister 2015; Gerkin and Castro 2015).
14. **"Gut serotonin controls your mood."** Most of the body's serotonin is made in the gut (about 90 to 95%), but it does not cross the blood-brain barrier. Gut-brain links run through nerves (vagus), immune signals and microbial metabolites, and are an active research area.
15. **"The tongue has taste zones."** False (Section 8).
16. **"Your brain is fully developed at 18" or "exactly at 25."** Both are oversimplified (Section 12).
17. **"Brain training games make you generally smarter."** They improve the trained task, but transfer to everyday cognition is weak (Simons et al. 2016, Psychological Science in the Public Interest).
18. **"The brain feels pain when cut."** Brain tissue has no pain receptors, which allows awake surgery. The meninges and vessels do have them.
19. **"Living brains are gray."** Living brain tissue is pinkish-beige because of blood. "Gray matter" describes preserved tissue.
20. **"Damaged brains can never recover."** Plasticity allows meaningful recovery after stroke and injury, though often incomplete.

### 17c. Glossary (one-sentence plain definitions)

- **Action potential:** A brief electrical pulse that travels along an axon.
- **Allocortex:** Older, simpler cortex with fewer than six layers, such as the hippocampus and piriform cortex.
- **Amygdala:** An almond-shaped cluster that flags important or threatening events.
- **Aphasia:** A language disorder caused by brain damage.
- **Apraxia:** Trouble performing learned movements even though the muscles work.
- **Arcuate fasciculus:** A curved white-matter bundle linking frontal and temporal language areas.
- **Association cortex:** Cortex that combines information rather than handling one raw sense.
- **Astrocyte:** A star-shaped glial cell that supports neurons and blood vessels.
- **Axon:** The long output cable of a neuron.
- **Basal ganglia:** Deep nuclei that help select and start movements and habits.
- **Blood-brain barrier:** A tight lining of brain blood vessels that controls what enters the brain.
- **Brainstem:** The stalk connecting the brain to the spinal cord, made up of the midbrain, pons and medulla.
- **Brodmann area:** A numbered cortex region defined by its cell layering.
- **Central sulcus:** The groove separating the frontal and parietal lobes, also called the fissure of Rolando.
- **Cerebellum:** The "little brain" at the back that fine-tunes movement and thought.
- **Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF):** Clear fluid that cushions and bathes the brain.
- **Choroid plexus:** Tissue in the ventricles that makes most CSF.
- **Circle of Willis:** A ring of arteries at the brain's base that links its blood supplies.
- **Connectome:** A complete map of connections in a nervous system.
- **Contralateral:** On the opposite side.
- **Corpus callosum:** The large bundle of fibers connecting the two hemispheres.
- **Cortex:** The thin, folded outer layer of gray matter.
- **Cranial nerves:** Twelve pairs of nerves that leave the brain directly.
- **Critical period:** A time window when experience shapes a brain system most strongly.
- **Decussation:** A place where nerve fibers cross the midline.
- **Default mode network:** Brain regions most active during rest and inward thought.
- **Dendrite:** A branching input part of a neuron.
- **Dopamine:** A chemical messenger involved in movement, motivation and learning.
- **Dura mater:** The tough outer covering of the brain.
- **Executive function:** Mental skills for planning, focusing and self-control.
- **fMRI:** Brain scanning that tracks blood oxygen changes linked to activity.
- **Fissure:** A deep groove in the brain.
- **Frontal lobe:** The front lobe, involved in movement, planning and personality.
- **GABA:** The main calming (inhibitory) chemical messenger in the brain.
- **Glia:** Non-neuron brain cells that support, protect and insulate.
- **Glutamate:** The main exciting chemical messenger in the brain.
- **Gray matter:** Brain tissue rich in neuron cell bodies and synapses.
- **Gyrus:** A ridge on the brain's surface.
- **Hemisphere:** One half of the cerebrum.
- **Hippocampus:** A seahorse-shaped structure key for new memories and navigation.
- **Homunculus:** A distorted body map showing how much cortex each body part gets.
- **Hypothalamus:** A small region controlling hunger, thirst, temperature and hormones.
- **Insula:** Hidden cortex inside the lateral fissure, involved in body feelings and taste.
- **Ipsilateral:** On the same side.
- **Lateral fissure:** The deep groove above the temporal lobe, also called the Sylvian fissure.
- **Lateralization:** When a function relies more on one hemisphere.
- **Lesion:** An area of damaged tissue.
- **Limbic system:** A loosely defined set of structures involved in emotion and memory.
- **Lobe:** One of the large divisions of each hemisphere (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital).
- **Medulla:** The lowest brainstem part, which controls breathing and heart rate.
- **Meninges:** The three protective membranes around the brain.
- **Microglia:** Immune cells of the brain.
- **Midbrain:** The top part of the brainstem, involved in eye movement, reflexes and dopamine.
- **Motor cortex:** Cortex that sends movement commands.
- **Myelin:** A fatty insulation around axons that speeds signals.
- **Neuron:** A nerve cell that sends electrical and chemical signals.
- **Neurogenesis:** The birth of new neurons.
- **Neuroplasticity:** The brain's ability to change with experience or injury.
- **Neurotransmitter:** A chemical that carries signals across a synapse.
- **Nucleus (brain):** A cluster of neuron cell bodies deep in the brain.
- **Occipital lobe:** The back lobe, mainly for vision.
- **Oligodendrocyte:** A glial cell that makes myelin in the brain.
- **Optic chiasm:** The X-shaped crossing of optic nerve fibers.
- **Parietal lobe:** The top-back lobe for touch, space and attention.
- **Pons:** The brainstem "bridge" linking cortex to cerebellum.
- **Prefrontal cortex:** The front part of the frontal lobe for planning and self-control.
- **Proprioception:** Sensing where your body parts are without looking.
- **Purkinje cell:** A large, branching output neuron of the cerebellar cortex.
- **Receptor:** A protein or cell that detects a signal.
- **Reflex:** An automatic, rapid response to a stimulus.
- **Retinotopic map:** A brain map that preserves the layout of the visual scene.
- **Somatosensory:** Relating to body senses like touch and position.
- **Spinal cord:** The nerve column carrying signals between brain and body.
- **Stroke:** Brain injury from a blocked or burst blood vessel.
- **Sulcus:** A groove between gyri.
- **Synapse:** The junction where neurons communicate.
- **Temporal lobe:** The side lobe for hearing, language and memory.
- **Thalamus:** A relay hub that passes most senses to the cortex.
- **Tonotopic map:** A brain map arranged by sound frequency.
- **Tract:** A bundle of axons in the brain or spinal cord.
- **Ventricle:** A fluid-filled space inside the brain.
- **White matter:** Brain tissue made mostly of myelinated axons.

- Neuron counts come from four male brains (Azevedo et al. 2009). Animal numbers come mostly from one lab's method. Present them as approximate.\[7\]
- Statistics (stroke, tumors, BCI participant counts, prizes) are dated to the latest available publication as of October 2026. Review them at least yearly. GBD 2023 stroke estimates may replace GBD 2021.
- Several "modern" findings are single studies or single participants: SCAN, Dumitru 2025, Mousley 2025, BCI speech results, Miao 2024. Label them that way in voice scripts.
- BrainFacts.org and QBI material should be paraphrased only. Nothing here copies their wording.
- Source URLs are not listed here. They should be attached in the citation pass for each named source (author, year, journal) given above.
- Medical boundary: nothing in this report is diagnostic or treatment guidance. Stroke BE FAST and "call emergency services" are the only action statements, and they are safety information.
