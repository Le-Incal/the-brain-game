# Synaptic Plasticity

**Say it:** plasticity (plas-TISS-ih-tee), potentiation (po-ten-shee-AY-shun), NMDA (N-M-D-A), AMPA (AM-pa), Hebb (HEB)

## In one breath

When you practise something, the connections between your brain cells tend to get stronger. That is how your brain learns, and it is likely happening right now as you play.

## The tour

- **What it is:** The ability of synapses to become stronger or weaker depending on how they are used.
- **How it functions:** When two connected neurons are active together in the right pattern, the connection between them strengthens. Connections used in other patterns can weaken. Stronger connections pass messages more easily next time.
- **What it's connected to:** Every synapse in the brain can change, but the hippocampus (memory), cortex (skills and knowledge) and cerebellum (movement learning) are the most studied.
- **What it controls:** Learning, memory, skill, habit, and recovery after injury.

Memorable fact: this game is plasticity in action. Placing a word on the right region, again and again, likely strengthens the connections that link that word to that place.

## Short-term vs long-term

- **Short-term plasticity:** Lasts milliseconds to minutes. Includes facilitation (a quick second spike gets a bigger response) and depression (a burst tires the synapse). Helps the brain filter and track rhythm.
- **Long-term plasticity:** Lasts hours to a lifetime. Long-term potentiation (LTP) strengthens a synapse; long-term depression (LTD) weakens it.

## For the expert

- **Hebb (1949):** Proposed that a connection strengthens when one cell repeatedly helps fire another. The slogan "cells that fire together wire together" came later (Carla Shatz, 1992).
- **Discovery of LTP:** Bliss and Lømo, 1973, in the rabbit hippocampus (perforant path to dentate gyrus), after high-frequency stimulation.
- **Mechanism (classic NMDA-dependent LTP):** NMDA receptors are coincidence detectors. They open only if glutamate is bound and the postsynaptic membrane is already depolarised, which pops out a magnesium block. Large calcium influx activates CaMKII, which inserts more AMPA receptors, strengthening the synapse. Modest calcium rises activate phosphatases instead and produce LTD.
- **Phases:** Early LTP (minutes to hours) needs no new protein. Late LTP needs gene expression and protein synthesis (PKA, CREB) and structural growth of dendritic spines.
- **Timing rule:** Spike-timing-dependent plasticity: if the input fires just before the output (within about 20 ms), the synapse strengthens; just after, it weakens (Markram 1997; Bi and Poo 1998).
- **Other forms:** Cerebellar LTD at parallel fibre to Purkinje cell synapses (Ito) underlies motor learning. mGluR-dependent LTD. Homeostatic scaling keeps total activity stable.
- **Beyond synapses:** Memory also involves changes in neurons' intrinsic excitability, structural rewiring, and engram cell assemblies. LTP is the leading model, not the whole story.
- **Recovery:** After stroke or injury, surviving circuits reorganise through these mechanisms, which is why rehabilitation and practice matter.

## Common questions

- **Can adults still learn new things?** Yes. Plasticity continues throughout life, though some kinds are strongest in childhood.
- **Why does practice work?** Repetition strengthens the specific connections a skill uses.
- **Why do I forget?** Unused connections can weaken, and new learning can interfere with old.

## Careful with

- Talk about plasticity, not new neurons, when explaining learning. Plasticity is solid science; adult neurogenesis in humans is debated.
- LTD is not just disuse. It needs specific activity patterns too.
