# Neurotransmitters

**Say it:** neurotransmitter (NOOR-oh-TRANS-mit-er), acetylcholine (a-SEE-til-KOH-leen), glutamate (GLOO-ta-mate), GABA (GAB-a), dopamine (DOH-pa-meen), serotonin (ser-oh-TOH-nin), noradrenaline (nor-a-DREN-a-lin)

## In one breath

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that neurons use to talk to each other. Some say "go," some say "stop," and some change the mood of a whole area.

## The tour

- **What they are:** Chemicals released by a neuron at a synapse that change the activity of the next cell.
- **How they function:** An electrical pulse arrives at the axon tip, transmitter is released into the gap, it binds receptor proteins on the next cell, and is then cleared away or recycled. Whether the effect excites or inhibits depends on the receptor, not only on the chemical.
- **What they're connected to:** Receptors on neurons, muscles and glands; transporters that recycle them; and enzymes that break them down.
- **What they control:** Movement, sensation, mood, sleep, reward, attention, learning and memory, and automatic body functions.

Memorable fact: the first neurotransmitter was discovered in a frog's heart. In 1921 Otto Loewi showed that fluid from a stimulated heart could slow a second heart. The chemical was acetylcholine.

## The main ones

| Transmitter | Main role | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| Glutamate | Main excitatory ("go") signal in the brain | Used by about 80% of cortical neurons. Central to learning (see synaptic_plasticity.md). |
| GABA | Main inhibitory ("stop") signal in the brain | Made from glutamate. Many sedatives act on GABA receptors. |
| Glycine | Inhibitory, mainly spinal cord and brainstem | Also a required co-agonist at NMDA receptors. |
| Acetylcholine | Muscle contraction; attention and memory in the brain | The transmitter at every skeletal nerve-muscle junction. Basal forebrain acetylcholine neurons decline in Alzheimer's. |
| Dopamine | Movement, motivation, reward learning | From the substantia nigra and VTA in the midbrain. Loss causes Parkinson's. |
| Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) | Alertness, arousal, attention; "fight or flight" in the body | From the locus coeruleus in the pons. Main sympathetic nerve transmitter. |
| Serotonin | Mood, sleep, appetite, pain | Brain serotonin comes from the raphe nuclei in the brainstem. |
| Histamine | Wakefulness | From the hypothalamus. Why older antihistamines make you sleepy. |

**Neuropeptides:** more than 100 known, including endorphins and other opioid peptides (pain relief), substance P (pain signalling), and oxytocin. Often released alongside a fast transmitter.

**Unusual messengers:** nitric oxide (a gas), endocannabinoids, and ATP/adenosine. Endocannabinoids and nitric oxide can travel backward across the synapse.

## For the expert

- **Classes by role:** Fast transmitters act at ionotropic receptors within milliseconds in a single synapse. Neuromodulators (dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, acetylcholine in many circuits) act through metabotropic receptors over larger areas and longer times, changing how circuits respond.
- **Co-transmission:** Many neurons release more than one transmitter. Dale's principle (a neuron releases the same set of transmitters at all its terminals) still largely holds; the popular misreading of it as "one neuron, one transmitter" is what turned out wrong.
- **Clearance:** Reuptake transporters (target of SSRIs and many stimulants), enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholinesterase, target of Alzheimer's cholinesterase inhibitors), and diffusion.

## On the model

Not visible. Midbrain and brainstem nuclei (near region 20) are the source of most dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline, broadcasting to the whole brain.

## Common questions

- **What chemical makes you happy?** No single one. Mood depends on many transmitters and circuits. Dopamine is more about wanting and learning than pleasure itself.
- **What does caffeine do?** Adenosine builds up in the brain while you're awake and makes you sleepy. Caffeine blocks the receptors adenosine acts on.
- **Is serotonin made in the gut?** Most of the body's serotonin is, but it cannot cross the blood-brain barrier. The brain makes its own.

## Careful with

- Never say gut serotonin controls mood directly.
- Do not call dopamine "the pleasure chemical."
- Do not say acetylcholine loss causes Alzheimer's. It is a consequence; amyloid and tau pathology are central.
- Do not give medical or medication advice.
