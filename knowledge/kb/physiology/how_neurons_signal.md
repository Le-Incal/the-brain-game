# How Neurons Signal

**Say it:** action potential (AK-shun po-TEN-shal), depolarisation (dee-po-lar-eye-ZAY-shun), threshold (THRESH-hold), millivolt (MIL-ih-volt)

## In one breath

Neurons talk using quick electrical zaps and tiny squirts of chemicals. A zap runs down the neuron, and at the end it sends chemicals across a gap to wake up the next neuron.

## The tour

- **What it is:** Two linked steps. An action potential is a brief electrical spike that travels along the axon. A synapse is the junction where that spike is handed to the next cell, usually as a chemical message.
- **How it functions:** A neuron receives hundreds to thousands of inputs. Some push it toward firing (excitatory), others hold it back (inhibitory). If the push wins by enough to cross a threshold, the neuron fires. The spike races down the axon, and at each terminal it releases neurotransmitter, which crosses the gap and changes the electrical state of the next cell.
- **What it's connected to:** Inputs mostly on dendrites and the cell body; outputs at axon terminals onto other neurons, muscles or glands.
- **What it controls:** Every message in the nervous system. The balance of excitation and inhibition decides what gets passed on.

Memorable fact: a spike is all or nothing, like a camera flash. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger spike; it makes more spikes, faster.

## For the expert

- **Resting potential:** About −70 mV (range roughly −60 to −80 mV by cell type), maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and potassium leak channels.
- **Threshold:** About −55 mV (roughly −50 to −55 mV).
- **The spike:** Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, peaking near +30 to +40 mV. Na⁺ channels then inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open, repolarising the membrane, followed by a brief afterhyperpolarisation. Duration about 1 to 2 ms. A refractory period prevents backward travel.
- **Initiation:** Usually at the axon initial segment.
- **Conduction:** About 0.5 to 2 m/s in thin unmyelinated fibres, up to about 120 m/s with myelin and saltatory conduction.
- **Chemical synapse:** The spike opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the terminal. Ca²⁺ triggers vesicles to fuse and release transmitter into the cleft, about 20 to 40 nm wide. Transmitter binds receptors on the receiving cell. Synaptic delay is about 0.5 to 1 ms.
- **Postsynaptic potentials:** Excitatory (EPSPs) and inhibitory (IPSPs) summate in space and time.
- **Receptors:** Ionotropic (fast, open a channel directly) and metabotropic (slower, act through second messengers).
- **Electrical synapses:** Gap junctions pass current directly, used where speed or synchrony matters.
- **Scale:** About 80% of cortical neurons are excitatory (glutamate). A typical cortical pyramidal neuron has several thousand to over ten thousand synapses. Estimates for total synapses in the brain range from about 100 to 1,000 trillion.
- **History:** Hodgkin and Huxley worked out the ionic basis of the action potential in the squid giant axon (Nobel Prize 1963).

## Common questions

- **Is the brain electric?** Partly. Signals travel along neurons as electrical pulses carried by charged particles, then cross synapses as chemicals.
- **How fast can a neuron fire?** Many can fire hundreds of times per second.
- **Why does a sleeping foot tingle?** Pressure on the nerve disrupts signalling; as it recovers, nerves fire erratically.

## Careful with

- Calcium is Ca²⁺, not Ca⁺.
- Do not say spikes get bigger with stronger stimuli. They get more frequent.
