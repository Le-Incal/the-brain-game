# The Hindbrain

**Say it:** pons (PONZ), medulla oblongata (meh-DULL-a ob-long-GAH-ta), cerebellum (ser-eh-BELL-um), Purkinje (pur-KIN-jee), vermis (VER-mis)

## In one breath

The hindbrain is at the bottom and back of the brain. It keeps you breathing and your heart beating without you thinking about it, and the wrinkly "little brain" helps you balance, move smoothly and even think.

## The tour

- **What it is:** The lowest part of the brain. It has three parts: the pons, the medulla oblongata, and the cerebellum. The pons and medulla, with the midbrain, form the brainstem.
- **How it functions:** The brainstem runs life-support automatically and carries every signal travelling between brain and body. The cerebellum compares what you meant to do with what your body is actually doing, and corrects the difference.
- **What it's connected to:** The spinal cord below; the midbrain and forebrain above; the inner ear's balance organs; and cranial nerves V to XII.
- **What it controls:** Breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, swallowing, coughing, sneezing and vomiting reflexes, sleep and arousal, balance, posture, coordination, timing, and motor learning.

Memorable fact: the cerebellum is only about 10% of the brain's volume but holds about four out of every five of its neurons.

## The three parts

- **Medulla oblongata:** About 3 cm long, continuous with the spinal cord. Houses the breathing and cardiovascular centres. Most motor fibres cross from one side to the other here, which is why each hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body. Cranial nerves IX, X, XII arise here.
- **Pons:** Latin for "bridge." Its bulge carries fibres from the cortex to the cerebellum. It helps regulate breathing rhythm and sleep, including dreaming (REM) sleep. Cranial nerve V (trigeminal, facial sensation and chewing) exits from it; VI, VII and VIII exit at its lower border.
- **Cerebellum:** Latin for "little brain." Two hemispheres joined by the worm-shaped vermis. Tightly folded surface (folia). Grey matter outside, white matter inside in a tree-like pattern called the arbor vitae, "tree of life."

## For the expert

- **Cerebellar anatomy:** Three lobes (anterior, posterior, flocculonodular); deep nuclei (dentate, interposed, fastigial) are its main outputs, though some flocculonodular and vermal Purkinje cells project directly to the vestibular nuclei. Purkinje cells are the sole output of the cortex and are inhibitory. A single Purkinje cell receives up to about 200,000 parallel-fibre synapses.
- **Functional zones:** Flocculonodular lobe for balance and eye movements (vestibulocerebellum); vermis and intermediate zones for posture and ongoing limb control (spinocerebellum); lateral hemispheres for planning, timing and cognition (cerebrocerebellum), looping with prefrontal and parietal cortex via the pons and thalamus.
- **Cognition and emotion:** The lateral cerebellum participates in language, working memory, timing, and emotional regulation. Damage can cause cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (Schmahmann): difficulties with planning, language and emotional control.
- **Laterality:** Each cerebellar hemisphere coordinates the same side of the body (ipsilateral), unlike the cortex.
- **Brainstem lesions** can be catastrophic because tracts and vital centres are packed tightly; locked-in syndrome results from damage to the front of the pons.

## Common questions

- **What part of the brain keeps me breathing?** The medulla, with help from the pons.
- **Is the cerebellum just for balance?** No. It fine-tunes every movement and also helps with timing, language, attention and emotion.
- **Why can't I tickle myself?** Scientists think the cerebellum predicts the feeling of your own touch and tells the touch areas to turn it down.
- **Why does alcohol make people wobbly?** The cerebellum is especially sensitive to alcohol.

## Careful with

- Never describe the cerebellum as "only balance." This is a standing project rule.
- The pons relays cortex to cerebellum. It does not link the two hemispheres.
