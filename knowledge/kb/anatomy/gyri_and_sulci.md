# Gyri and Sulci

**Say it:** gyrus (JY-rus), gyri (JY-rye), sulcus (SUL-kus), sulci (SUL-kye), Sylvian (SIL-vee-an), calcarine (KAL-ka-rine), gyrification (jy-rih-fih-KAY-shun), lissencephaly (liss-en-SEF-a-lee)

## In one breath

The bumps on the brain are called gyri and the grooves between them are called sulci. Folding lets a big, flat sheet of brain fit inside your head, like crumpling a towel into a cup.

## The tour

- **What they are:** The cerebral cortex is a thin sheet that folds into ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci). The deepest grooves are called fissures.
- **How they function:** Folding packs far more cortex, and so far more neurons, into the skull. About two-thirds of the cortex is hidden inside the grooves.
- **What they're connected to:** Underneath every gyrus is white matter, carrying fibres to and from that patch of cortex.
- **What they control:** The folds themselves do not control anything. They are landmarks. Some grooves mark borders between areas with very different jobs, which is why surgeons and scientists use them like street names.

Memorable fact: flattened out, the human cortex would cover about three and a half sheets of printer paper.

## For the expert

- **Scale:** Folding roughly triples cortical surface area, to about 2,200 cm² in humans. Cortical thickness is about 1.5 to 4.5 mm on MRI, averaging about 2.5 mm.
- **Why the cortex folds:** No single theory explains it. Proposals include differential growth of upper and lower cortical layers, tension from white matter axons, and expansion of outer radial glia in the outer subventricular zone, which drives tangential growth. The old idea that the skull forces folding is weakened by experiments where gyri form even when the skull is partly removed.
- **Sulcal types:** Limiting sulci divide functionally different cortex (central sulcus). Axial sulci run along fast-growing regions (calcarine). Operculated sulci separate two areas at their lips while a third area lies hidden in their own walls (the lunate sulcus, with striate cortex buried inside).
- **Timing:** Primary sulci form before birth; secondary and tertiary sulci form late in pregnancy and after. The first appear late in the third fetal month; most primary sulci appear between about weeks 16 and 30. The calcarine appears around week 16 and the central sulcus around week 20.
- **Variability:** The lateral fissure is usually continuous; the central, calcarine and collateral sulci are rarely interrupted; the postcentral and frontal sulci are often broken. Fine fold patterns differ between individuals, even identical twins.
- **Comparative:** Humans, cats, ferrets, whales and seals have folded (gyrencephalic) brains. Mice, rats, marmosets and manatees have smooth (lissencephalic) brains. Several smooth-brained lineages lost folding during evolution, often alongside smaller body size.
- **When folding fails:** Lissencephaly ("smooth brain") results from failed neuronal migration. Isolated lissencephaly sequence affects about 1 in 100,000 newborns; within that form, LIS1 (PAFAH1B1) causes more than half of cases and DCX about 10%, with TUBA1A a smaller share, all linked to microtubules. Most affected children have seizures, often severe.

## On the model

The hatching shader follows the real gyri and sulci of this model, and region boundaries were snapped toward sulci during painting. When the agent names a fold, it should face the matching region from the table above. The insula and Heschl's gyri cannot be shown because they are buried.

## Careful with

- "Wrinkles equal intelligence" is a myth. Within humans, folding is at best weakly linked to intelligence and cannot tell you how smart someone is. Across species, folding tracks brain size more than cleverness.
- The angular gyrus caps the superior temporal sulcus. The supramarginal gyrus caps the lateral fissure. Some sources mix these up.
- Do not say "we only see a third of the brain." We see about a third of the cortex's surface; the rest of it is folded inside the grooves.
