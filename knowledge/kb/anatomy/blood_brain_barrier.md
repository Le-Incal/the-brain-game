# Blood-Brain Barrier

**Say it:** endothelial (en-doh-THEE-lee-al), pericyte (PAIR-ih-site), astrocyte (AS-tro-site)

## In one breath

The blood-brain barrier is like a very careful security guard in the walls of the brain's blood vessels. It lets in food and oxygen and keeps out germs and many harmful chemicals.

## The tour

- **What it is:** A selective wall formed by the cells lining the brain's capillaries, sealed together by tight junctions so substances cannot slip between them.
- **How it functions:** Oxygen, carbon dioxide and small fat-soluble molecules pass through freely. Nutrients like glucose need special transporter proteins to get in. Most other things are kept out.
- **What it's connected to:** The brain's capillary network, surrounding astrocytes and pericytes, and the neurons those vessels feed.
- **What it controls:** What reaches brain tissue from the blood: it blocks toxins and pathogens, and keeps hormone, ion and water levels around neurons stable.

Memorable fact: it was discovered with dye. Injected into the blood, the dye stained nearly every organ except the brain and spinal cord.

## For the expert

- **Neurovascular unit:** Endothelial cells with tight junctions, a basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocyte end-feet wrapping the vessel. Astrocytes and pericytes help induce and maintain the barrier.
- **Transport:** Glucose crosses via GLUT1 because it is polar, not because it is large. Amino acids, some hormones and other nutrients use their own carriers. Efflux pumps actively push many drugs back out.
- **Gaps by design:** Circumventricular organs (such as the area postrema, the "vomiting centre," and parts of the hypothalamus) lack a full barrier so they can sample the blood.
- **History:** Paul Ehrlich's dye experiments in the 1880s showed the brain stayed unstained, though he thought the tissue simply didn't take up the dye. Max Lewandowsky (1900) proposed that brain capillaries restrict passage. Edwin Goldmann injected dye into the blood (1909) and then into the CSF (1913); the second stained the brain alone, showing a true barrier. Lina Stern named it the "hemato-encephalic barrier" around 1921. Electron microscopy in the 1960s (Reese and Karnovsky) revealed the tight junctions.
- **Drug delivery:** Most candidate brain drugs cannot cross. Strategies include "Trojan horse" drugs attached to molecules that have transporters, and focused ultrasound with gas microbubbles to open the barrier briefly. Ultrasound results in Alzheimer's models are mostly preclinical; human trials are early.
- **Disease:** Multiple sclerosis involves barrier breakdown letting immune cells into the CNS. Some bacteria causing meningitis pry open tight junctions. Stroke and tumors can disrupt the barrier, which is why contrast agents "light up" some tumors on scans.

## On the model

Not visible. It is a microscopic feature of nearly every brain capillary, so it exists throughout all 20 painted regions. The small exceptions are the circumventricular organs, deep inside.

## Common questions

- **Why can't medicines just get into the brain?** The barrier blocks most molecules, which protects the brain but makes treating brain diseases hard.
- **How does the brain get its food?** Glucose and oxygen pass through, glucose with the help of special carrier proteins.
- **Does caffeine get through?** Yes, it is small and crosses easily. So do alcohol and nicotine.

## Careful with

- Do not say "nothing" gets through. It is selective, not sealed.
- Do not present ultrasound barrier-opening as an available Alzheimer's treatment.
