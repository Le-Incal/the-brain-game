# Corpus Callosum

**Say it:** corpus callosum (KOR-pus ka-LOH-sum), splenium (SPLEE-nee-um), genu (JEE-noo), commissure (KOM-ih-shur)

## In one breath

The corpus callosum is a thick bridge of nerve fibres that connects the left and right halves of your brain, so they can share what they know.

## The tour

- **What it is:** A broad, flat band of white matter at the bottom of the longitudinal fissure, joining the two hemispheres. It is the largest white matter structure in the brain.
- **How it functions:** It carries signals back and forth so each half knows what the other is seeing, feeling and doing.
- **What it's connected to:** Matching areas of cortex in the left and right hemispheres, front to back.
- **What it controls:** It does not control anything by itself. It coordinates the two halves, so you can, for example, name an object you feel with your left hand (language lives mostly on the left).

Memorable fact: it carries roughly 200 million nerve fibres in a band about 10 centimetres long.

## For the expert

- **Parts, front to back:** rostrum, genu, body, splenium. The genu links prefrontal cortex; the splenium links occipital and posterior temporal cortex (vision).
- **Other commissures:** anterior commissure (temporal lobes, olfaction) and posterior commissure (pupillary reflexes) also cross the midline.
- **Split-brain surgery (callosotomy):** A last-resort treatment for severe epilepsy, stopping seizures from spreading across. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga studied these patients from the 1960s; Sperry shared the 1981 Nobel Prize. Patients speak and behave normally day to day. Under lab conditions, an object shown only to the right hemisphere cannot be named aloud but can be picked out by the left hand. The left hemisphere's tendency to invent explanations for actions it did not plan is called the "interpreter."
- **Agenesis:** Partial or complete absence, about 1 in 4,000 people, arising around weeks 12 to 16 of gestation. Effects range from minimal to severe. Some people compensate well through alternative pathways. DCC gene mutations are one cause and are linked to mirror movements.
- **Evolution:** Present only in placental mammals.
- **Fibre count:** Estimates range from about 200 to 300 million; 200 to 250 million is the most cited.

## On the model

Hidden. It lies at the floor of the gap between the hemispheres, visible only if the brain were cut down the middle. The agent can face the superior view, point into the longitudinal fissure and say "deep down there, my corpus callosum joins my two halves." Region 16 (cingulate) arches directly above it on the medial surface.

## Common questions

- **What happens if the corpus callosum is cut?** People usually seem completely normal. Special tests reveal that each half can know things the other half doesn't.
- **Do the two halves of the brain talk to each other?** Constantly, mainly through the corpus callosum.
- **Are there left-brained and right-brained people?** No. Both halves work together in everyone, all the time.

## Careful with

- Do not say split-brain patients lose their speech. They can speak; they cannot verbally report what only the right hemisphere saw.
- Do not use split-brain findings to support "left logical, right creative" personalities.
