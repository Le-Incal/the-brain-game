# Glia

**Say it:** glia (GLEE-a), astrocyte (AS-tro-site), oligodendrocyte (oh-lih-go-DEN-dro-site), microglia (my-kro-GLEE-a), ependymal (eh-PEN-dih-mal), Schwann (SHWON)

## In one breath

Glia are the brain's helper cells. They feed neurons, clean up waste, fight germs, and wrap wires so messages move fast.

## The tour

- **What they are:** All the non-neuron cells of the nervous system. "Glia" comes from the Greek for "glue," because they were once thought to just hold neurons together.
- **How they function:** Glia mostly do not fire electrical spikes like neurons, but they actively shape how neurons work: controlling chemicals around synapses, insulating axons, defending the brain, and helping build it during development.
- **What they're connected to:** Neurons, synapses, axons, blood vessels, and the fluid-filled ventricles.
- **What they control:** Brain immunity and clean-up, synapse pruning, the chemical environment around synapses, signal speed, blood flow and barrier upkeep, and cerebrospinal fluid flow.

Memorable fact: there are roughly as many glia as neurons in the brain, not ten times more as once believed.

## The types

**In the brain and spinal cord**
- **Astrocytes:** Star-shaped. Mop up excess neurotransmitter and potassium at synapses, store and supply fuel (how much they feed neurons directly is still debated), wrap blood vessels with their end-feet to support the blood-brain barrier, and link neural activity to local blood flow. Part of the "tripartite synapse."
- **Oligodendrocytes:** Wrap CNS axons in myelin. One cell can myelinate segments of many axons. Myelin is why white matter is white.
- **Microglia:** Immune cells. Patrol, detect injury and infection, clear debris, and prune unneeded synapses during development. Overactive microglia contribute to inflammation in Alzheimer's.
- **Ependymal cells:** Line the ventricles and central canal and move cerebrospinal fluid with beating cilia. A specialised form in the choroid plexus produces most CSF.
- **Radial glia:** Stem-like cells in the developing brain that produce neurons and glia, and act as scaffolding young neurons climb to reach their place in the cortex.
- **Oligodendrocyte precursor (NG2) cells:** About 5 to 8% of CNS cells; can become new oligodendrocytes throughout life.
- **Specialists:** Bergmann glia in the cerebellum (region 19), Müller glia in the retina, tanycytes in the hypothalamus.

**Outside the brain and spinal cord**
- **Schwann cells:** Myelinate peripheral nerves, one cell per segment, and guide regrowth after injury.
- **Satellite glia:** Surround neuron cell bodies in ganglia; may contribute to chronic pain.
- **Enteric glia:** Support the gut's nervous system.

## For the expert

- **Origin:** Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes come from neural progenitors. Microglia do not: they come from yolk-sac immune precursors that migrate into the brain early in development (Ginhoux et al. 2010).
- **Signalling:** Glia signal by calcium waves and release gliotransmitters. Some oligodendrocyte precursors produce spike-like events.
- **Glymphatic system:** Proposed astrocyte-dependent clearance of waste, more active during sleep. Still actively debated.
- **Tumors:** Most primary brain cancers (gliomas, including glioblastoma) arise from glial lineage cells, because neurons rarely divide.

## Common questions

- **If glia don't send messages, why do they matter?** Without them, neurons would starve, misfire, and be defenceless.
- **What are brain immune cells?** Microglia.
- **Why do most brain cancers come from glia?** Glia and their precursors keep dividing; most neurons do not. (The most common brain tumour overall, meningioma, grows from the brain's covering, not from glia.)

## Careful with

- Do not say "10 glia for every neuron."
- Do not say microglia come from neural stem cells.
- Do not call glia "just glue."
